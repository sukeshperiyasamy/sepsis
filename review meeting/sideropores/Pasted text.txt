# SERS-Based Detection of Bacterial Siderophores from Human Blood: End-to-End Analytical Pipeline Design

## Executive overview

Enterobactin (Ent), salmochelin (Gly-Ent), and yersiniabactin (Ybt) are high-affinity siderophores that underpin iron acquisition and virulence for Enterobacteriaceae and related pathogens, and are actively engaged by host defenses such as lipocalin-2 (Lcn2).  Their coordinated detection directly from human blood with surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) would provide a label-free, rapid readout of pathogen iron-scavenging activity and metallophore speciation, complementing culture and LC‑MS/MS diagnostics.  This report designs a complete, four-stage diagnostic pipeline—Release, Recovery, Enhancement, Identification—integrating siderophore coordination chemistry, blood sample preparation, nanoplasmonic engineering, and chemometric/machine learning analysis, with orthogonal validation and point-of-care (POC) considerations.[1][2][3][4][5][6]


## Biology of target siderophores and host interactions

Enterobactin is a tris-catecholate trilactone with an extremely high Fe(III) binding affinity (overall log β ≈ 49–52), enabling iron scavenging even under the iron-restricted conditions of host tissues.  Salmochelins are C‑glucosylated enterobactins that retain the catecholate scaffold but evade Lcn2 binding, while Ybt is a mixed phenolate–thiazolinone siderophore associated with the Yersinia high-pathogenicity island and FyuA transporter.  Lcn2 binds catecholate siderophores such as Ent and blocks iron acquisition, whereas salmochelin and Ybt are Lcn2-resistant and support virulence in serum, lung, and urinary tract infections.[7][8][2][9][10][11][6][12][1]

The three siderophores differ in metal selectivity: Ent is highly specific for Fe(III), whereas Ybt forms stable complexes with Fe(III), Cu(II), Ni(II), Co(II), and other metals, and copper–Ybt does not compete with Fe(III)–Ybt for uptake.  These differences in ligand donor set and metal coordination geometry are a key handle for SERS-based spectral discrimination and for interpreting metal-speciation effects in blood samples.[13][11]


## Stage 1 – Release

### 1.1 Liberation from lipocalin-2 and protein complexes

Lcn2 binds ferric enterobactin and related catecholate siderophores with nanomolar affinity and sequesters them in plasma and mucosal secretions as part of innate immunity.  Salmochelin avoids sequestration by C‑glucosylation of 2,3‑dihydroxybenzoyl‑L‑serine moieties, and Ybt does not bind Lcn2, so these ligands are more likely to circulate protein-free or bound to other carriers such as albumin and transferrin.  In serum, Ybt-dependent growth can be inhibited by transferrin, indicating competition between Fe–Ybt and host Fe-ligands and suggesting labile exchange equilibria.[14][2][9][15][7][1]

For analytical release, the key constraints are: (i) breaking Lcn2–Ent complexes without degrading the trilactone backbone, and (ii) dissociating Fe(III) while preserving the organic scaffold for SERS and LC‑MS detection. Protein denaturation and pH shifts are the primary tools, and their design must respect the different acid sensitivities of catecholate versus hydroxamate/mixed siderophores.[16][17]


### 1.2 Acidification and Fe–siderophore dissociation

Ferric enterobactin is extremely stable at neutral pH as a tris-catecholate complex; however, stepwise protonation of the catecholate oxygens and a coordination shift to a tris-salicylate form occur upon acidification, lowering the Fe(III)/Fe(II) reduction potential and facilitating reductive iron release.  Spectroscopic and electrochemical measurements show that protonated Fe(III)–Ent can be reduced near physiological reductant potentials, whereas the fully deprotonated complex at neutral pH is resistant to reduction.  These data support a low‑pH pre-treatment (pH ≈ 2–3) that both denatures proteins (Lcn2, albumin, transferrin) and destabilizes Fe(III)–Ent for subsequent reduction and ligand recovery.[18][19]

Hydroxamate siderophores such as aerobactin show slightly higher Fe(III) complex stability at acidic pH relative to catecholates, and catecholate siderophore production itself is favored at neutral to alkaline pH, consistent with weaker catecholate complexes at low pH.  Ybt, with a phenolate–thiazolinone donor set, forms stable complexes with Fe(III) and several divalent metals; LC‑MS and UV–vis studies indicate that Cu(II)–Ybt, Fe(III)–Ybt, and other complexes are acid-sensitive but not immediately destroyed at moderately low pH, especially in the absence of strong nucleophiles.[20][11][13][16]


### 1.3 Stability of siderophore scaffolds under acidic conditions

The trilactone ester bonds of enterobactin are susceptible to acid-catalyzed hydrolysis, which is in fact exploited by bacteria to release iron intracellularly; technical guides and mechanistic studies show increasing hydrolysis rates as pH decreases below neutral.  Nonetheless, structural analyses indicate that the catecholate aromatic rings remain intact and the hydrolysis products (e.g., 2,3‑dihydroxybenzoyl‑serine oligomers) maintain characteristic catechol vibrational signatures, which are still quantifiable by colorimetric catechol assays and LC‑MS.  Salmochelin shares the same trilactone backbone but has glycosylated catechols; glycosidic bonds may also hydrolyze at very low pH and elevated temperature, so pH ≈ 2 with short exposure and cooled conditions is preferable to minimize backbone cleavage.[21][22][23][19][18]

Ybt is more acid-labile, with reports of acid sensitivity prompting the avoidance of trifluoroacetic acid in HPLC purification and use of milder formic acid modifiers during LC‑MS.  This suggests that release conditions should be optimized separately for catecholate and Ybt analysis or chosen as a compromise: moderate acidification (pH 2–3), limited incubation time (≤15 min on ice), and immediate neutralization before solid-phase extraction.[24]


### 1.4 Recommended release protocol

An optimized release step for blood/plasma samples can be structured as follows, balancing dissociation efficiency against scaffold integrity:

- Adjust plasma or serum to pH 2.0–2.5 with concentrated HCl or formic acid on ice, targeting rapid protein denaturation and Fe(III)–Ent protonation while minimizing extended exposure.[23][18]
- Incubate for 10–15 min at 0–4 °C to promote dissociation of Lcn2–Ent and Fe–siderophore complexes; optionally add a mild reductant (e.g., ascorbate) post-acidification to reduce Fe(III) and further destabilize complexes, guided by potentials measured for protonated Fe–siderophore complexes.[19][18]
- Immediately neutralize to pH 6–7 with cold ammonium bicarbonate or similar volatile buffer prior to extraction to slow trilactone and glycosidic hydrolysis and protect Ybt.[24]
- Clarify by centrifugation to remove precipitated proteins and proceed to the Recovery stage.

This protocol can be tuned in pilot experiments by monitoring intact siderophore signals by LC‑MS/MS across pH/time conditions.


## Stage 2 – Recovery

### 2.1 Protein precipitation versus liquid–liquid extraction

Classical protein precipitation with organic solvents (methanol, acetonitrile) efficiently removes the bulk of plasma proteins and is widely used in bioanalysis and metabolomics; methanol precipitation regularly achieves >90% recovery for many small molecules and is faster and simpler than liquid–liquid extraction (LLE), though it does not efficiently remove phospholipids.  LLE with ethyl acetate or chloroform–methanol can extract siderophores from culture supernatants and environmental samples, but recovery is strongly dependent on pH, ionic strength, and the presence of metal complexes; Ybt has been extracted into ethyl acetate for LC‑MS, whereas its acid sensitivity required omission of strong acids such as TFA.[25][26][27][28][24]

For small, moderately polar siderophores in plasma, precipitation followed by solid-phase extraction (SPE) provides better control over matrix effects than LLE alone, while minimizing sample handling.[26][29]


### 2.2 Solid-phase extraction and sorbent selection

Oasis HLB is a water-wettable, polymeric reversed-phase sorbent composed of divinylbenzene and N‑vinylpyrrolidone, enabling both hydrophobic π–π and hydrophilic interactions and supporting extraction of acidic, basic, and neutral analytes in a simple load–wash–elute protocol.  In contrast, C18 is a purely hydrophobic silica-based sorbent that interacts poorly with highly polar or ionized analytes and does not remove phospholipids efficiently.  Application notes show that Oasis PRiME HLB removes >90% of phospholipids and fats from plasma and meat extracts in a pass-through configuration, whereas C18 removes only fats and leaves phospholipids, leading to matrix effects and instrument fouling.[30][31][32][33][34][35]

For catecholate/salicylate siderophores and Ybt:

- Oasis (PRiME) HLB cartridges or µElution plates are preferred for broad retention across protonation states and for simultaneous phospholipid removal.[32][34][30]
- C18 may co-retain more hydrophobic components and fewer polar siderophore species, risking poor recovery and higher background.


### 2.3 Phospholipid and matrix interference removal

Phospholipids are among the most abundant endogenous small molecules in plasma and cause strong ion suppression in LC‑MS and competitive adsorption on SERS substrates, as shown in exposomics workflows and plasma SPE optimization studies.  Oasis PRiME HLB and related phospholipid-removing sorbents can eliminate >90% of phospholipids in a simple pass-through or load–wash protocol, dramatically improving sensitivity and reducing matrix effects.[31][33][36][29][32]

To specifically prepare samples for SERS, where hemoglobin and purine metabolites dominate spectra of whole blood and plasma, further conditioning is beneficial: SERS spectra of plasma after AuNP addition show intense hypoxanthine bands after short storage times, indicating strong surface selectivity for purines released from erythrocytes.  Combining phospholipid-removal SPE with a size- and polarity-focused wash step (e.g., aqueous 5–10% methanol with 0.1% formic acid) can reduce purine and protein carryover while retaining siderophores, which are more hydrophobic than free purine bases.[37]


### 2.4 Recommended recovery workflow

A practical, high-throughput recovery scheme after acidic release and neutralization is:

1. Perform protein precipitation by adding 3–4 volumes of ice-cold methanol or acetonitrile, vortexing, and centrifuging to pellet precipitated proteins; collect supernatant.[25][26]
2. Dilute supernatant with water to ≤10–20% organic content and adjust pH to ~3–4 to favor retention of siderophores on HLB while minimizing non-specific binding of salts.[34][30]
3. Load onto Oasis PRiME HLB cartridges; discard flow-through if using retention mode. Wash with aqueous buffer (e.g., 5–10% methanol with 0.1% formic acid) to remove phospholipids and highly polar metabolites, leveraging the high phospholipid removal capability of PRiME HLB.[33][31][32]
4. Elute siderophores with 80–100% methanol or acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid or ammonia, collect eluate, and split into two portions: one for LC‑MS/MS quantification and one to be dried/reconstituted in SERS-compatible buffer for the enhancement and identification stages.

This strategy favors Oasis HLB over C18 because of its broader retention profile and superior matrix clean-up for phospholipids, critical for both LC‑MS and SERS reproducibility.[30][32][34]


## Stage 3 – Enhancement

### 3.1 SERS substrates: AgNPs, AuNPs, and nanostars

SERS enhances Raman scattering by orders of magnitude via localized surface plasmon resonances of nanostructured metals, typically silver or gold.  Gold and silver nanoparticles, roughened electrodes, and nanostructured films have all been used for bacterial detection in complex matrices including blood and urine, often reaching detection limits near or below 10⁴ CFU/mL.  For small-molecule detection, colloidal AgNPs provide strong enhancements but can suffer from oxidative instability and aggregation; AuNPs are more biocompatible but sometimes less enhancing at 785 nm.[38][3][4][39][40]

Nanostars—with sharp tips and high-curvature regions—provide intense electromagnetic “hot spots” and tunable plasmon resonances in the near-IR, making them well suited for 785 nm portable Raman systems.  Recent work demonstrates that Au nanostar–Ag satellite or Au/Ag nanostar hybrids can dramatically boost SERS signals and maintain high reproducibility across multiple spots and samples, with relative standard deviations below 5–6%.[41][42][43][44]


### 3.2 Hotspot engineering and aggregation control

Hot spots arise in nanogaps between adjacent particles or between particle tips and metallic films; their density and uniformity largely determine SERS sensitivity and reproducibility.  Strategies to create controllable hot spots include:[40]

- Salt-induced aggregation of colloidal AgNPs/AuNPs, adjusting electrolyte concentration to create interparticle gaps of a few nanometers without causing macroscopic precipitation.[45][40]
- Directed assembly of core–satellite or nanostar–satellite architectures (e.g., Au nanostar@Ag satellites mediated by dithiols) that embed analytes within engineered nanogaps.[42][43]
- Immobilization of nanostars on solid supports (glass, SiO₂) with controlled surface coverage, as used for protein SERS and bacterial mapping.[44][38]

Because catecholate siderophores can bind strongly to metal oxide and metal surfaces through their dihydroxybenzoyl groups, as shown by ATR‑IR studies of enterobactin adsorption on TiO₂, AlOOH, and Cr oxide, they are expected to chemisorb preferentially onto Ag/Au nanostructures and localize at hot spots.  However, strong binding can also orient molecules in specific configurations and potentially alter relative band intensities, so calibration with standards is required.[46]


### 3.3 Magnetic nanoparticle-based enrichment

Magnetic nanoparticle (MNP)-based SERS substrates enable simultaneous separation, enrichment, and SERS readout in a single workflow, significantly improving selectivity and throughput for bioanalytical assays.  MNPs can be coated with noble metals (Au, Ag) or decorated with plasmonic satellites to form magnetically manipulable SERS tags that capture analytes via specific recognition ligands (e.g., aptamers, antibodies, or siderophore-binding domains).[47][48]

Recent reviews highlight MNP-based SERS substrates for detection of proteins and pathogens, while specific aptamer–MNP systems have been developed to capture siderophore–Fe³⁺ complexes and transduce binding via optical or electrochemical signals.  For siderophore analysis in blood, two approaches are promising:[49][47]

- **Non-specific enrichment:** Use bare or polymer-coated magnetic cores decorated with Ag/Au shells, exploit general affinity of catecholates and heterocyclic ligands for noble metals, and magnetically separate from residual matrix.
- **Specific capture:** Functionalize MNPs with Lcn2 or anti-siderophore antibodies to selectively bind Ent or Ybt complexes, then read out SERS signals on the same particles.


### 3.4 Recommended enhancement strategy

To balance performance and practicality for POC-compatible SERS:

- Develop colloidal Au nanostar or AuNS@Ag hybrid substrates with optimized plasmon resonance near 785 nm, following established synthesis and characterization protocols.[41][42][44]
- For laboratory development, use solution-phase mixing: combine reconstituted SPE eluate with AuNS@Ag colloid plus controlled NaCl or MgSO₄ concentration to induce moderate aggregation and hot spot formation; record spectra once the aggregation kinetics stabilize (≈1–5 min).[40][45]
- For future clinical deployment, transition to solid-supported or paper-based SERS chips with immobilized nanostars and magnetic capture layers, leveraging work on paper-based SERS chips and handheld-compatible bimetallic substrates.[50][39][41]

Careful optimization of nanoparticle concentration, salt, pH, and incubation time is required to minimize spectral variability and nanoparticle–ligand degradation.


## Stage 4 – Identification

### 4.1 Raman and SERS signatures of siderophores

Resonance Raman spectroscopy of ferric enterobactin and related catecholate complexes reveals strong benzene-ring and catecholate modes, with patterns similar to N‑methyl‑2,3‑dihydroxybenzamide and distinct from simple catechol, confirming binding through phenolate oxygens.  Other catechol-type siderophores, such as anguibactin, exhibit resonance Raman bands around 1367, 1447, 1469, and 1538 cm⁻¹ associated with catechol ring modes and Fe(III)–catecholate coordination, indicating that catecholate vibrational fingerprints are robust across siderophores.[51][52]

Ybt, by contrast, contains phenolate and thiazolinone rings; LC‑MS and spectroscopic analyses highlight UV–vis and MS signatures of metal–Ybt complexes, and coordination chemistry studies show that Cu(II)–Ybt and Fe(III)–Ybt complexes differ in geometry and exchange kinetics.  These structural differences are expected to manifest in SERS spectra as variations in ring-stretching and C–S/C–N vibrational bands, facilitating differential assignment when coupled with LC‑MS-informed calibration.[11][13]

In practice, pure standard SERS spectra of metal-free Ent, salmochelin, and Ybt, as well as their Fe³⁺, Cu²⁺, and mixed complexes, should be acquired under the same nanoparticle and buffer conditions used for blood samples to build a reference spectral library.


### 4.2 Spectral interferences from hemoglobin and purine metabolites

Normal Raman spectra of whole blood at 785 nm are dominated by oxyhemoglobin bands, whereas SERS spectra of whole blood on Au nanoparticle/SiO₂ substrates arise primarily from blood plasma, not red cells, under typical preparation conditions.  Over time, SERS spectra of stored blood become dominated by hypoxanthine bands originating from purine degradation, with similar temporal behavior in isolated plasma; hypoxanthine and other purines interact strongly with AuNPs and generate intense SERS signals.[53][54][55][37]

Hemoglobin itself can be strongly enhanced on Ag or AgO SERS substrates, yielding rich heme marker bands that can interfere with siderophore detection if red cells are not adequately removed or lysed.  Purine metabolites (adenine, hypoxanthine, xanthine) also have distinct SERS signatures and are frequently used as reporters for bacterial identification in SERS-based blood assays, highlighting the need to account for overlapping peaks and potential confounders.[56][57][58][59][37]

To mitigate these interferences:

- Use plasma or serum after erythrocyte removal and SPE-based phospholipid and metabolite clean-up.[29][26][37]
- Include purine standards in the spectral library and treat their bands as separate components in chemometric models, rather than attempting to eliminate them entirely.
- Exploit differences in excitation profile (e.g., potential resonance of Fe–siderophore complexes in the UV–visible) by adjusting laser wavelength if feasible.


### 4.3 Chemometric and classical machine learning models

SERS spectra of bacteria and blood are high-dimensional and often non-linearly separable. Numerous studies have applied chemometrics (PCA, hierarchical clustering, linear discriminant analysis) and machine learning (support vector machines, random forests, convolutional neural networks) to classify bacterial species, antimicrobial resistance, and infection status from Raman/SERS data.  Recent work on bloodstream infections shows that deep learning architectures such as Vision Transformers and CNNs trained on thousands of SERS spectra from clinical blood samples can achieve >99% accuracy in predicting Gram type, species, and resistance phenotypes.[60][61][3][62][63][64][5][38]

For siderophore-focused detection, a hierarchical approach is appropriate:

- **Unsupervised exploration:** Use PCA and t‑SNE/UMAP to visualize spectral variance and identify clusters corresponding to Ent-rich, salmochelin-rich, Ybt-rich, and control spectra, while also revealing major variance contributions from purines and residual proteins.[3][60]
- **Supervised classification:** Train SVM, random forest, or gradient boosting models on known mixtures and clinical samples with LC‑MS/MS-labeled siderophore concentrations to classify samples by siderophore presence (binary) and relative dominance (multi-class), incorporating spectral preprocessing (baseline correction, normalization, peak picking).[62][3]
- **Quantitative regression:** Use partial least squares regression (PLSR) or deep learning regressors to map SERS spectral intensities to siderophore concentrations derived from LC‑MS/MS, enabling semi-quantitative readouts.


### 4.4 Deep learning and CNN-based identification

Deep learning has recently been applied to SERS spectra for rapid bacterial identification and antimicrobial resistance profiling, with CNNs and Siamese networks outperforming classical methods on noisy, variable spectra.  For example, a SERS chip plus Siamese neural network classifier achieved species- and strain-level discrimination at bacterial concentrations as low as 100 CFU/mL in synthetic urine.  Vision Transformer-based models trained on SERS spectra from clinical blood samples have also achieved >99% accuracy for rapid bloodstream infection classification.[61][64][62]

Adapting these architectures to siderophore detection requires:

- Curating large spectral datasets from standards, spiked plasma, and clinical samples with paired LC‑MS/MS and CAS/Arnow assay labels for Ent, salmochelin, and Ybt concentrations.[65][66][22]
- Including spectra with varying metal speciation (Fe³⁺, Cu²⁺, Ni²⁺, mixed states) to train the model to recognize speciation-dependent spectral shifts.[13][11]
- Incorporating data augmentation (spectral noise, baseline variation, intensity scaling) to mimic real-world instrument and sample variability, as done in recent sepsis and pathogen SERS–DL studies.[67][63][5]


## Metal speciation and its spectroscopic consequences

Ybt acts as a metallophore rather than a pure siderophore, forming stable complexes with Fe(III), Cu(II), Ni(II), Co(II), and Cr(III), all of which can be imported by FyuA in a TonB-dependent manner; Cu(II)–Ybt is structurally distinct and does not inhibit Fe(III)–Ybt uptake.  In vivo, copper–Ybt complexes have been detected in urine during infection, indicating that non-iron metals are physiologically relevant binding partners.  Coordination chemistry studies of Fe(III)–siderophore complexes show that protonation and ligand identity modulate reduction potentials and electronic transitions, directly affecting resonance enhancement conditions.[17][18][11][16][13]

For SERS, this implies that spectra may encode not only ligand identity but also metal identity and oxidation state. By calibrating SERS spectra of Ent, salmochelin, and Ybt with Fe(III), Cu(II), and Ni(II) in controlled buffer conditions and combining these with LC‑MS/MS speciation analysis, one can train models to infer metallophore speciation directly from SERS signals, potentially informing host–pathogen metal interactions and therapeutic strategies.


## Orthogonal validation: LC‑MS/MS and colorimetric assays

### 7.1 LC‑MS/MS quantification

LC‑MS/MS is the gold standard for sensitive and specific quantification of enterobactin, Ybt, and related siderophores in biological matrices.  Protocols for enterobactin typically use reversed-phase C18 columns with gradient elution and selected reaction monitoring of characteristic fragment ions, allowing ng/mL-level detection in culture supernatants and potentially in plasma after SPE clean-up.  Ybt has been quantified in high-cell-density fermentations and biological samples using LC‑MS, with titers up to ~67 mg/L in engineered E. coli and detection of Fe(III)–Ybt complexes by MS/MS.[28][21][65][24]

In the proposed pipeline, LC‑MS/MS serves several roles:

- Primary quantification of Ent, salmochelin, and Ybt in spiked and clinical blood samples for correlation with SERS intensities and model training.
- Assessment of recovery and degradation through the release and recovery steps by comparing pre- and post-treatment concentrations.
- Metal-speciation analysis by resolving Fe(III)–, Cu(II)–, and other metal–siderophore complexes based on mass shifts and fragmentation patterns.[24][13]


### 7.2 Universal and catechol-specific colorimetric assays

The Chrome Azurol S (CAS) assay is a universal colorimetric method that detects siderophores independent of structure by measuring competition for Fe(III) from a preformed Fe–CAS complex, causing a blue-to-orange color shift proportional to siderophore chelation.  CAS can be applied in solution for quantitative measurements or on agar plates for qualitative screening of siderophore-producing strains.[66][68][69][70]

For catecholate siderophores such as enterobactin and salmochelin, the Arnow assay and related catechol-specific colorimetric methods provide sensitive detection of catechol groups via nitration or Fe(III) reduction chemistry.  Arnow-based methods can detect micromolar levels of 2,3‑dihydroxybenzoic acid equivalents and are more sensitive than the classic Arnow nitration assay for certain catechols.[22][71][65]

In the SERS pipeline, CAS provides a total siderophore index, while Arnow and catechol-specific assays estimate catecholate contributions; together with LC‑MS/MS, these assays validate SERS-based classification and quantification, particularly in early development when SERS models are being calibrated.


## Optimized end-to-end analytical workflow

### Workflow overview

The most robust end-to-end pipeline for detecting Ent, salmochelin, and Ybt from human blood by SERS integrates the four stages as follows:

1. **Sample collection and preprocessing**
   - Collect EDTA or heparinized blood, separate plasma/serum promptly to minimize hemolysis and purine release.[26][37]
   - Optionally store at 4 °C for minimal time before processing, as hypoxanthine accumulates within 10–20 h and dominates SERS spectra.[37]

2. **Release**
   - Acidify plasma to pH 2–2.5 on ice for 10–15 min to dissociate Lcn2–Ent and Fe–siderophore complexes and denature proteins.[1][18][23]
   - Neutralize to pH 6–7 with volatile buffer; centrifuge to remove precipitated proteins.

3. **Recovery**
   - Perform protein precipitation with cold methanol/acetonitrile, centrifuge, and dilute supernatant.[25][26]
   - Load onto Oasis PRiME HLB SPE; wash to remove phospholipids and polar interferents; elute siderophores with high-organic solvent.[32][33][30]
   - Split eluate for LC‑MS/MS and SERS; evaporate and reconstitute the SERS fraction in aqueous buffer (e.g., 10–20% methanol, pH 7–8).

4. **Enhancement**
   - Mix reconstituted extract with Au nanostar or AuNS@Ag colloids under controlled aggregation conditions to create hot spots; alternatively, apply to a nanostar-based solid SERS chip.[42][44][41][40]
   - For future improvements, integrate magnetic Au/Ag-coated nanoparticles for simultaneous enrichment and SERS readout.[48][47]

5. **Identification and quantification**
   - Acquire SERS spectra with a 785 nm benchtop or handheld Raman spectrometer, using standardized acquisition parameters and internal standards where possible.[72][50][37]
   - Preprocess spectra (cosmic-ray removal, baseline correction, normalization) and apply chemometric and ML models trained on standard libraries and LC‑MS/MS-labeled samples to classify siderophore presence and approximate concentration, while controlling for purine and protein signatures.[64][60][3][62]
   - Confirm representative samples by LC‑MS/MS, CAS, and Arnow assays.


## Critical bottlenecks and mitigation strategies

### 9.1 Chemical stability and release conditions

- **Bottleneck:** Acidification required for Fe–siderophore dissociation risks hydrolysis of enterobactin/salmochelin trilactones and Ybt degradation.
- **Mitigation:** Optimize pH (2–3), temperature (0–4 °C), and exposure time; immediately neutralize and validate integrity by LC‑MS/MS.[18][23][24]

### 9.2 Matrix interference from hemoglobin and purines

- **Bottleneck:** Hemoglobin and purine metabolites dominate SERS spectra of blood and plasma, potentially masking siderophore bands.[58][56][37]
- **Mitigation:** Work with plasma/serum rather than whole blood, minimize storage time, use phospholipid-removing SPE and targeted washes, and model purine components explicitly in chemometric analyses.[29][26][37]

### 9.3 SERS substrate reproducibility

- **Bottleneck:** Batch-to-batch variation in colloidal nanoparticles and aggregation conditions leads to spectral variability, complicating quantitative analysis.[40]
- **Mitigation:** Transition from simple colloids to engineered nanostar-based substrates and bimetallic films with documented low RSD for SERS intensity; standardize synthesis, storage, and chip fabrication protocols; implement internal standards.[39][41][42]

### 9.4 Data scarcity for supervised learning

- **Bottleneck:** High-quality labeled datasets of siderophore SERS spectra in human blood are currently lacking.
- **Mitigation:** Systematically generate training sets from standards and controlled spiking experiments, pair with LC‑MS/MS and colorimetric assays, and leverage transfer learning from existing bacterial SERS datasets.[5][61][62][64]

### 9.5 Metal speciation complexity

- **Bottleneck:** Mixed Fe/Cu/Ni speciation of Ybt and potential metal exchange during sample handling complicate interpretation of SERS fingerprints.[11][13]
- **Mitigation:** Control metal content via chelex treatment and defined metal spikes in calibration, and use LC‑MS/MS to map speciation while training models to recognize speciation-dependent spectral changes.


## Feasibility for point-of-care diagnostics

SERS-based identification of bacteria and sepsis biomarkers has progressed from proof-of-concept to near-clinical performance, with several studies demonstrating rapid pathogen classification from blood and urine using SERS plus CNN/ViT models.  Portable and handheld Raman spectrometers, including devices specifically evaluated for POC applications, can now acquire high-quality spectra from SERS substrates, and reviews highlight modular, handheld systems for SERS-based POC testing in infection, cancer, and metabolic disease.[73][4][63][74][75][76][61][62][50][5]

Detection of low-abundance small-molecule biomarkers from minimally processed blood using portable SERS systems has also been shown feasible—for example, portable SERS combined with AuNPs can amplify weak plasma signals by ~67% and reveal otherwise hidden biomolecular bands.  Given these advances, a siderophore-focused SERS assay is technically feasible at the POC, provided that sample preparation can be miniaturized (e.g., microfluidic SPE and magnetic enrichment) and robust chemometric models are embedded into the device software.[72]

Remaining challenges include regulatory validation, large-scale clinical evaluation of diagnostic performance, and standardization of sample preparation across sites. Nonetheless, the trajectory of SERS–AI diagnostics suggests that a siderophore SERS assay could evolve from laboratory prototype to POC tool within a realistic translational timeframe, especially as part of integrated sepsis workups where SERS is already being explored.


## Recommendations for experimental design

### 11.1 Analytical development strategy

- **Phase 1 – Chemistry and substrate optimization**
  - Synthesize/obtain Ent, salmochelin, and Ybt (and selected analogs) and fully characterize their Raman/SERS spectra under controlled nanoparticle and buffer conditions with defined metal speciation.[51][46][13]
  - Systematically optimize release pH/time and SPE conditions using LC‑MS/MS to balance recovery vs. degradation, starting from the pH 2–3, cold, short-incubation regime.[65][18][24]
  - Screen AgNP, AuNP, and AuNS@Ag substrates (both colloidal and chip-based) for SERS intensity, reproducibility, and robustness in buffered standards and simple matrices.[39][41][42][40]

- **Phase 2 – Matrix integration and interference control**
  - Spike plasma/serum with siderophore standards at clinically relevant concentrations, apply the full release–recovery–enhancement workflow, and quantify recovery by LC‑MS/MS and CAS/Arnow assays.[66][22][65]
  - Characterize SERS spectra across storage times to understand the evolution of purine and other interfering signatures and refine SPE and washing steps accordingly.[26][29][37]

- **Phase 3 – Chemometrics and machine learning**
  - Build spectral libraries and use PCA/LDA and PLSR for initial classification and semi-quantification, using LC‑MS/MS and colorimetric data as ground truth.[60][3]
  - Train and evaluate SVM and CNN/ViT models for binary and multiclass siderophore detection, employing cross-validation and external validation on independent sample sets, analogous to recent SERS–DL sepsis and BSI studies.[63][61][62][5]

- **Phase 4 – Clinical feasibility**
  - Test the workflow on remnant clinical blood samples from patients with culture-proven infections where siderophore profiles (Ent/salmochelin/Ybt) are characterized by LC‑MS/MS and genomic assays.[8][6][11]
  - Evaluate assay performance in terms of limit of detection, specificity, time-to-result, and added diagnostic value relative to culture, PCR, and standard inflammatory markers.


### 11.2 Publication strategy

The project naturally decomposes into publishable units:

1. **Coordination chemistry and SERS of siderophore–metal complexes** (journal: Inorganic Chemistry, JACS, Chemical Science) – detailed vibrational assignments and speciation-dependent SERS signatures for Ent, salmochelin, and Ybt complexes.[46][51][13]
2. **Bioanalytical workflow for siderophores in blood** (journal: Analytical Chemistry, Analyst, ACS Sensors) – optimized release–SPE–LC‑MS/MS method with CAS/Arnow cross-validation and preliminary SERS data.[30][32][65][66]
3. **Machine-learning-enabled SERS detection of siderophores in plasma** (journal: ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces, Sensors and Diagnostics, Biosensors and Bioelectronics) – integrated AuNS@Ag substrates, chemometrics/CNN, and performance metrics in spiked and pilot clinical samples.[61][62][63]
4. **Point-of-care SERS platform for siderophore-guided sepsis diagnostics** (journal: npj Digital Medicine, Nature Communications, Clinical Chemistry) – demonstration of a portable system with microfluidic sample prep, handheld Raman, and embedded ML, evaluated in a clinical cohort.[75][73][50][5][72]

Coherent positioning within the growing literature on SERS-based bacterial detection, metallophore biology, and POC Raman diagnostics will help maximize impact and facilitate eventual clinical translation.
