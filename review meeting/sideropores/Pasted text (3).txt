Comprehensive Analytical Framework for the Extraction, Enrichment, and SERS-Based Detection of Bacterial Siderophores in Clinical Biofluids
The survival and proliferation of pathogenic bacteria within the human host are contingent upon their ability to navigate extreme nutritional iron limitation. While iron is abundant in the human body, it is strictly sequestered by high-affinity proteins such as transferrin in the blood, lactoferrin in mucosal secretions, and ferritin within intracellular compartments. To circumvent this host-mediated nutritional immunity, pathogens such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Salmonella enterica secrete siderophores—low-molecular-weight organic chelators that exhibit extraordinary affinities for ferric iron (Fe 
3+
 ). The analysis of these molecules, particularly enterobactin, yersiniabactin, and salmochelins, from complex biological matrices like blood, plasma, and serum is a critical objective for clinical microbiology and the development of rapid diagnostic platforms. Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) has emerged as a promising tool for the trace detection of these metabolites due to its high sensitivity and ability to provide molecular fingerprints, yet the transition from laboratory culture to clinical biofluids necessitates a sophisticated understanding of chemical speciation, matrix interference, and specialized extraction workflows.   

Molecular Architecture and Chemical Speciation of Target Siderophores
The analytical strategy for siderophore recovery is fundamentally dictated by the chemical structure and speciation of the target analytes. Siderophores are diverse in their chemical composition, typically employing catecholate, hydroxamate, or α-hydroxycarboxylate functional groups to hexacoordinate Fe 
3+
  in an octahedral geometry.   

Enterobactin and the Catecholate Class
Enterobactin (Ent) is the most potent iron chelator characterized to date, with a formation constant (K 
a
​
 ) for Fe 
3+
  of approximately 10 
52
 . Structurally, it is a cyclic triester formed from three L-serine residues, each coupled to a 2,3-dihydroxybenzoyl (DHB) moiety. The three catechol groups provide six oxygen donor atoms for iron coordination. In the host environment, enterobactin is found in two primary states: the iron-free (apo) form and the iron-laden (ferric or holo) form. The transition between these states involves significant changes in the electronic environment of the molecule. Apo-enterobactin exhibits a maximum UV absorption at 320 nm, while the ferric complex (Fe-Ent) shows characteristic peaks at 340 nm and 496 nm, representing ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT). This spectral shift is critical for analytical monitoring during extraction and purification.   

Salmochelins and the Stealth Mechanism
Salmochelins (variants S1, S2, and S4) represent an evolutionary adaptation to host immunity. Produced via the C-glucosylation of enterobactin by the iroB gene product, salmochelins contain glucose residues at the C5 position of the DHB rings. This modification is not merely a metabolic byproduct; it serves to prevent the sequestration of the siderophore by the human protein Lipocalin-2 (Lcn2), also known as siderocalin. Salmochelins exist in various forms, including the cyclic trimer (S4) and its linearized derivatives, such as the trimer (S2) and the monomer (S1). The presence of glucose residues increases the hydrophilicity of these molecules compared to the highly hydrophobic enterobactin, which significantly alters their partitioning behavior during liquid-liquid extraction and their retention on solid-phase extraction sorbent beds.   

Yersiniabactin and Quorum Sensing Dynamics
Yersiniabactin (Ybt) is a structurally distinct, mixed-type siderophore containing phenolate and thiazoline rings. Unlike the hexacoordinate catecholates, Ybt coordinates iron in a 1:1 stoichiometry with a distinct geometric arrangement. Beyond its role in iron scavenging, Ybt has been identified as an autoinducer in a density-dependent transcriptional cycle, functioning as a quorum-sensing molecule. In infections caused by uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), Ybt expression is preferentially stimulated during bacterial crowding, while enterobactin supports initial growth at low cell densities. This non-redundant functioning of siderophore systems implies that the analytical target may shift depending on the stage and severity of the infection.

Matrix Interference in Human Biofluids
The clinical determination of siderophores is complicated by the extreme heterogeneity of blood, plasma, and serum. Interference arises from three primary sources: chemical sequestration by host proteins, physical fouling of sensor surfaces, and spectral overlap from matrix components.   

Protein Sequestration and the Lipocalin-2 Barrier
The primary biological interference in the analysis of enterobactin is Lipocalin-2 (Lcn2), an innate immune protein secreted by neutrophils and mucosal cells. Lcn2 binds ferric enterobactin with a dissociation constant (K 
D
​
 ) of approximately 0.4 nM, which is comparable to the affinity of the bacterial enterobactin receptor FepA. This binding effectively "masks" enterobactin from standard extraction protocols, as the protein-siderophore complex remains in the aqueous phase during solvent partitioning. The calyx of Lcn2 is lined with positively charged residues (R81, K125, K134) that form π-cation interactions with the negatively charged ferric siderophore. Successful recovery of enterobactin from clinical samples thus requires the disruption of this high-affinity interaction through metal dissociation or protein denaturation.   

The Protein Corona and SERS Surface Fouling
For downstream detection using SERS, the presence of high-abundance proteins like albumin and globulins in serum creates a significant challenge known as the fouling effect. When plasmonic nanoparticles (e.g., gold or silver) are introduced into a biological fluid, proteins rapidly adsorb to the metal surface, forming a "protein corona". This corona physically blocks target siderophores from reaching the "hotspots"—regions of high electromagnetic field enhancement between nanoparticles. Studies using bovine serum albumin (BSA) and lactalbumin have demonstrated that the protein corona increases the distance between the metal surface and the analyte, significantly reducing the chemical and electromagnetic enhancement factors. Furthermore, proteins can inhibit the target-induced aggregation of nanoparticles, which is often necessary to generate the most intense SERS signals.

Extraction Workflows and Sample Pretreatment
Efficient extraction of siderophores from blood-derived matrices requires a tiered approach that prioritizes protein removal and analyte stabilization.

Selective Lysis and Washing for Blood Samples
When analyzing whole blood or blood-culture samples, the presence of red blood cells (RBCs) and their primary component, hemoglobin, represents the most significant spectral interference. Hemoglobin dominates the SERS spectrum, obscuring the trace signals of bacterial metabolites. A robust pretreatment protocol involves the selective lysis of RBCs using ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) buffer, followed by ultrasonication to facilitate sonoporation. This process releases the bacterial contents into the supernatant while maintaining the integrity of the bacterial pellet. Subsequent washing steps—typically three cycles of centrifugation at 19,500 g followed by resuspension in deionized water—are essential to remove the residual hemoglobin and plasma proteins.   

Protein Precipitation Strategies
For plasma and serum, protein precipitation (PPT) is the most common pretreatment step. PPT involves altering the solubility of proteins by adding miscible organic solvents such as methanol (MeOH) or acetonitrile (ACN). These solvents destroy the hydration layer of proteins and reduce the repulsive forces between molecules, causing them to precipitate out of solution.   

Research indicates that methanol-based precipitation is particularly effective for SERS detection in plasma. For example, the detection of the antibiotic ceftriaxone in spiked plasma improved from a limit of 70 μg/mL in raw plasma to 20 μg/mL following methanol pretreatment. Methanol reduces "molecular crowding" and the formation of the protein corona at SERS hotspots by removing interfering substances such as uric acid and glutathione. However, the choice of solvent affects the morphology of the precipitate; ACN tends to produce larger particulates that are easier to remove by filtration, whereas MeOH produces finer precipitates that may require higher centrifugation speeds or more complex filtration setups.   

Liquid-Liquid Extraction (LLE)
Liquid-liquid extraction is a standard method for isolating catecholate siderophores from aqueous phases. The protocol generally involves:   

Acidification: The sample is acidified to a pH of approximately 2.0 using concentrated hydrochloric acid (HCl). This step is crucial as it protonates the catechol hydroxyl groups, increasing the solubility of the siderophore in organic solvents and disrupting its interaction with host sequestering proteins.   

Solvent Partitioning: An equal volume of ethyl acetate is added, and the mixture is shaken vigorously. Catecholates like enterobactin and salmochelins partition into the ethyl acetate phase.   

Phase Separation and Concentration: The organic phase is collected and evaporated to dryness using a rotary evaporator or vacuum centrifuge. The resulting residue can then be reconstituted in a SERS-compatible solvent or analyzed via LC-MS.   

Solid-Phase Extraction (SPE)
Solid-phase extraction provides higher throughput and better recovery for a wide range of siderophores compared to LLE. Hydrophilic-lipophilic balance (HLB) sorbents, such as Oasis HLB, are superior to traditional C18 reversed-phase sorbents for this application. HLB sorbents are made from a macroporous copolymer of [poly(vinylbenzene-co-N-vinylpyrrolidone)], which allows for the retention of both polar and non-polar analytes.   

A simplified 3-step SPE protocol on Oasis HLB involves loading the sample (after dilution with 4% phosphoric acid to ensure protonation), washing with a weak organic solvent (e.g., 5% MeOH), and eluting with 100% methanol or acetonitrile. This method has achieved recoveries exceeding 80% for diverse biological metabolites with minimal signal suppression in subsequent mass spectrometry analysis.

Metal Dissociation and Apo-Scaffold Stabilization
The high thermodynamic stability of the Fe 
3+
 −siderophore complex (logβ 
110
​
 >30) means that the metal is rarely dissociated under physiological conditions. However, for analytical purposes—especially for confirming the intactness of the siderophore scaffold via NMR or SERS—the iron must be removed.   

Chemical Acidification and Displacement
The addition of strong acids (pH < 2) is the primary chemical route for iron dissociation from catecholates. In clinical serum samples, this acidification also serves to release enterobactin from Lcn2. The protonation of the catechol oxygens destabilizes the coordination environment, allowing the iron to be released into the aqueous medium while the apo-siderophore is recovered in the organic phase. In laboratory settings, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) or other synthetic chelators are sometimes used to compete for the metal, though their high affinity can make the subsequent separation of the synthetic chelator from the natural siderophore difficult.   

Enzymatic Dissociation Pathways
Pathogenic bacteria have evolved sophisticated enzymatic machinery to release iron from siderophores once internalized. The iroA gene cluster, common in Salmonella and K. pneumoniae, encodes several such enzymes.   

IroD: A cytoplasmic esterase that hydrolyzes the ferric form of salmochelins (MGE, DGE, TGE) and enterobactin into DHB-Ser monomers. It is highly efficient at processing the ferric complexes, suggesting that iron release is coupled with the degradation of the siderophore scaffold.   

IroE: A periplasmic esterase that prefers apo-siderophores. It typically hydrolyzes the cyclic trilactone only once, producing linearized trimers.   

Fes: The primary esterase for ferric enterobactin in E. coli. While Fes is highly effective for Fe-Ent, it cannot efficiently hydrolyze ferric salmochelins, necessitating the IroD enzyme in iroA-carrying strains.   

From an analytical perspective, these enzymes offer a highly specific method for dissociating iron without the need for harsh chemicals that might degrade other metabolites in a clinical sample.   

Enrichment Techniques for Spectroscopic Sensitivity
Given that siderophores are often present at trace levels (nanomolar concentrations) in clinical samples, enrichment is necessary to reach the detection limits of standard SERS or LC-MS platforms.   

Affinity Capture with Lipocalin-2 and Aptamers
Engineered Lipocalin-2 variants can be used as high-affinity capture reagents. One such variant was designed to bind metal complexes with a dissociation constant of 400 pM, demonstrating the potential for using these proteins to "pull down" specific siderophores from plasma. Similarly, aptamers—synthetic DNA or RNA sequences—can be conjugated to magnetic nanoparticles to create "capture probes". A dual-recognition SERS biosensor using wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) for broad-spectrum bacterial recognition and specific antibodies or aptamers for siderophore capture has shown detection limits as low as 7 CFU/mL in bacterial cultures.   

Paper-Based SERS and Enrichment
A novel approach to enrichment involves the use of paper-based SERS biosensors (enPSERS). By decorating filter paper with graphene oxide-gold nanostar (GO-GNS) hybrids, researchers have integrated sample enrichment and detection into a single platform. Graphene oxide provides high surface area and allows for enrichment through π−π and electrostatic interactions, while the gold nanostars provide the necessary SERS enhancement. Such platforms have been used to detect bilirubin in blood serum with detection limits as low as 0.436 μM and could be adapted for the rapid detection of siderophores in resource-limited settings.   

Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS) Compatibility
The compatibility of siderophores with SERS is determined by their ability to adsorb onto metallic nanostructures and the resulting vibrational enhancement.   

Plasmonic Nanoparticles and Hotspots
Gold and silver nanoparticles are the most widely used SERS substrates. Silver nanostructures generally provide higher enhancement factors (up to 10 
6
 ) but are prone to oxidation, whereas gold nanoparticles are more chemically stable and easier to functionalize with capture ligands. The most intense SERS signals are generated at "hotspots"—nanogaps (2–10 nm) between particles or at the tips of sharp nanostructures like gold nanostars. Optimal SERS performance is typically observed with nanoparticles in the 50–60 nm size range, which aligns well with the common excitation wavelengths of 633 nm and 785 nm.

Spectral Identification and Biomarkers
The SERS spectra of bacterial cultures are often characterized by dominant peaks at 725-735 cm 
−1
 , which are attributed to the ring-breathing modes of purine derivatives like adenine and hypoxanthine. These molecules are secreted by bacteria as metabolic indicators and their intensity can be used to monitor bacterial viability and antibiotic susceptibility. Siderophores contribute specific vibrational bands that allow for their differentiation:   

Enterobactin: Shows strong peaks in the 1200–1400 cm 
−1
  region corresponding to the catecholate ring vibrations and the C-O stretching of the serine backbone.   

Salmochelin: Exhibits additional features related to the glucose residues, particularly C-H and C-OH vibrations that are absent in the enterobactin spectrum.   

Yersiniabactin: Shows characteristic thiazoline ring modes and salicylate-like vibrations near 1000 cm 
−1
  and 1300 cm 
−1
 .   

Validation Approaches and Quantitative Analysis
For clinical implementation, the results obtained from SERS-based siderophore analysis must be validated using established quantitative techniques.

LC-MS/MS: The Reference Standard
Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) remains the "gold standard" for the definitive identification and quantification of siderophores in complex samples. LC-MS/MS offers unmatched sensitivity and specificity, allowing for the separation of closely related metabolites like the different glucosylation states of salmochelins (MGE, DGE, TGE). Validation involves comparing the SERS signal intensities at specific characteristic peaks with the molar concentrations determined by LC-MS/MS. Typical mobile phases for LC-MS analysis of siderophores include water and acetonitrile, both containing 0.1% formic acid, using a C18 or HSS T3 column for reversed-phase separation.   

The Arnow and CAS Assays
Traditional colorimetric assays serve as useful secondary validation tools. The Arnow assay is a semi-quantitative method specific for catechol-containing compounds. It relies on the reaction of catechols with nitrite-molybdate in acidic conditions, followed by the addition of sodium hydroxide to produce a red color measurable at 510–515 nm. Quantification is performed using 2,3-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHBA) as a standard.   

The Chrome Azurol S (CAS) assay is a universal screening method for siderophores. It involves a competition between the siderophore and the ferric complex of the CAS dye for iron; when the siderophore removes the metal, the dye changes from blue to orange. While simple and effective for culture supernatants, the CAS assay requires modifications for clinical samples, such as adding adsorbent resins to neutralize the toxic effects of the detergent hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (HDTMA) on certain microbes.

Conclusions and Clinical Implications
The analytical pipeline for the detection of enterobactin, salmochelin, and yersiniabactin from blood and serum represents a vital frontier in infectious disease diagnostics. Successful detection hinges on a comprehensive understanding of the siderophores' chemical speciation—particularly the role of glucosylation in evading host Lipocalin-2—and the development of extraction workflows that can effectively remove interfering serum proteins and hemoglobin.

Liquid-liquid extraction following acidification remains a robust method for catecholate recovery, while hydrophilic-lipophilic balance (HLB) solid-phase extraction offers superior versatility for high-throughput clinical applications. The integration of these extraction methods with surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) provides a rapid, label-free diagnostic platform capable of identifying bacterial pathogens and their virulence factors in as little as five hours directly from blood samples.

The future of this field lies in the development of integrated lab-on-a-chip platforms that can automate the RBC lysis, protein precipitation, and SERS detection steps. Furthermore, the use of engineered capture proteins and aptamers will enhance the selectivity of these sensors in the complex environment of the human biofluid. As antimicrobial resistance continues to rise, the ability to rapidly identify pathogens through their specific siderophore metabolic signatures will be a cornerstone of personalized and effective antibiotic therapy. The transition from bulk laboratory analysis to localized, surface-enhanced molecular sensing represents a paradigm shift in our ability to monitor the host-pathogen iron competition in real-time.
