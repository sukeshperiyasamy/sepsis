Why Siderophores Are Absent from the SERS Literature

My searches across three rounds found zero papers reporting SERS spectra of enterobactin, aerobactin, yersiniabactin, or pyoverdine. The siderophore literature is rich in biology (iron uptake mechanisms, Trojan horse drug conjugates) but the spectroscopic characterization is limited to UV-Vis, fluorescence, and EPR — not Raman or SERS. The likely reasons:

Fluorescence interference: Pyoverdine is inherently fluorescent (quantum yield ~0.2–0.4), which would overwhelm Raman signals under standard conditions. This is a general problem — fluorescence can be 10⁴× stronger than Raman scattering. Strategies to suppress it include longer-wavelength excitation (785–1064 nm), time-gated detection (Kerr gating suppresses fluorescence by exploiting the ~ns lifetime difference from Raman's ~fs timescale), shifted-excitation Raman difference spectroscopy, and computational methods like multivariate curve resolution.
Weak intrinsic Raman cross-sections compared to phenazines: Pyocyanin's phenazine ring system is highly conjugated and planar, yielding intense resonance-enhanced Raman bands. Catechol groups (in enterobactin) do have Raman-active modes — ring breathing (~1150–1200 cm⁻¹), C–O stretching (~1250–1300 cm⁻¹), and O–H bending — and should exhibit charge-transfer enhancement on Ag/Au surfaces via catechol–metal coordination. However, catechol-iron complexation (the functional state of siderophores during infection) would alter the Raman signature substantially. Capurso et al. (2024) confirmed distinct Raman signatures for catechol-iron coordination polymers using both experiment and DFT, which at least establishes that iron-catechol complexes are Raman-characterizable.
Low circulating concentrations: Siderophores in blood during infection are typically in the nM–low µM range, requiring SERS enhancement factors ≥10⁶ for detection. This is achievable with optimized substrates (pyocyanin detection already reaches 0.05 nM), but no one has tried it for siderophores.

Of these, staphyloxanthin is probably the most immediately actionable target for SERS-based S. aureus detection in sepsis: it's a carotenoid pigment unique to S. aureus with exceptionally strong resonance Raman bands (the carotenoid ν₁/ν₂/ν₃ triplet at ~1005/1155/1520 cm⁻¹ is among the strongest Raman signatures in biology), and it's already detectable in single-cell Raman experiments. The challenge is that it's membrane-associated rather than freely secreted, but membrane vesicles shed during infection could carry it into blood.

Yersiniabactin is particularly interesting for K. pneumoniae sepsis detection because hypervirulent strains produce it abundantly, and its thiazoline ring system should have distinct Raman bands in the 650–700 cm⁻¹ region (C–S stretching) that wouldn't overlap with common blood matrix signals. Kumar et al. (2024) showed that K. pneumoniae variably produces four siderophores (enterobactin, salmochelin, aerobactin, yersiniabactin) depending on virulence.

Molecular Features Favoring SERS Enhancement

From the charge-transfer SERS literature, the key features for strong chemical enhancement on Au/Ag substrates are:

Aromatic N-heterocycles (pyridine, purine, indole rings): These dominate label-free bacterial SERS because their lone-pair electrons facilitate charge transfer to/from the metal surface. This is why adenine and hypoxanthine are such reliable SERS signals.
Catechol groups: The ortho-dihydroxy arrangement enables strong bidentate coordination to metal surfaces, creating a charge-transfer pathway. This should make enterobactin highly SERS-active — but the iron-loaded form (the biologically relevant state) would have different adsorption geometry since the catechols are already coordinated to Fe³⁺.
Extended conjugated π-systems (phenazines, polyenes, tripyrroles): These provide large Raman polarizability derivatives and enable resonance Raman when excitation matches an electronic absorption band.
Thiol/thioether groups: Strong Au–S bonds ensure proximity to the surface; relevant for yersiniabactin's thiazoline rings.
The hierarchy of expected SERS enhancement for the candidate molecules, based on molecular features alone: pyocyanin (phenazine, already proven) > staphyloxanthin (polyene, resonance-enhanced) > violacein/prodigiosin (conjugated heterocycles) > enterobactin (catechol charge-transfer) > yersiniabactin (thiazoline + phenolate) > aerobactin (hydroxamate, weakest).

Key Gaps Summary

The most striking gap is the complete absence of SERS work on siderophores for any diagnostic application. Given that enterobactin is produced by virtually all Enterobacteriaceae during bloodstream infection, and that catechol–metal charge transfer is well-established in SERS theory, this represents a genuine opportunity. The main technical barriers are fluorescence (for pyoverdine), iron competition for catechol binding sites (for enterobactin on Au/Ag), and low concentrations. A 785 nm excitation SERS experiment with a high-enhancement substrate (e.g., silver nanorod arrays, which have already achieved sub-nM detection for pyocyanin and purines) would be the logical starting point.

This synthesis draws from three search rounds but is still a survey of what my queries captured — a systematic review of catechol-metal SERS interactions or a DFT-guided prediction of siderophore Raman spectra would require deeper work.
