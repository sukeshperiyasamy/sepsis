This search added substantial depth to each of the secreted biomarker categories you asked about. Combined with the earlier results, here's a synthesis organized by your target categories.

1. Phenazines — Pyocyanin (PYO)
Pyocyanin is the most extensively studied secreted bacterial metabolite for SERS detection. It's a P. aeruginosa-specific virulence factor/quorum sensing molecule with strong Raman-active phenazine ring vibrations, making it an ideal SERS target.

Atta & Vo-Dinh (2023, Analytical Chemistry) achieved the lowest LOD I found: 0.05 nM using sharp-branched gold nanostars (GNSs) in solution phase, with direct detection in drinking water, saliva, and urine at 0.4 nM using a portable Raman instrument — no sample pretreatment required.
Liu et al. (2024, Analyst) developed a SERS chip with nano-silver substrate and micro-chamber array for in situ monitoring of PYO during P. aeruginosa biofilm growth, achieving LOD of 1 nM with a dynamic range of 1 nM–100 µM. Results were validated against mass spectrometry.
Kim et al. (2022, Sensors and Actuators B) designed a paper-based 3D SERS substrate with one-pot Au electrodeposition that captures PYO directly from culture medium without purification, enabling real-time monitoring of bacterial signaling during growth.
Tanaka et al. (2021, Analyst) built a portable SERS sensor for wound fluid, comparing AgNPs, AgAuNPs, and AuNPs. AgNPs achieved 1.1 µM LOD, covering the clinically relevant wound infection range of 1–50 µM PYO. Swab-based collection completed detection within 5 minutes.
El-Said et al. (2024, Biosensors) used gold/anodic aluminum oxide (Au/AAO) substrates with controlled roughness, achieving 96 nM LOD with a 1–9 µM linear range.
Wang & Vikesland (2023, Environ. Sci. Technol.) showed PYO dominates the metabolite SERS signal from ampicillin-resistant P. aeruginosa and that exogenous PYO can enhance ampicillin tolerance in co-cultured E. coli — demonstrating SERS as a tool for studying resistance mediation through secreted metabolites.
PYO is by far the most "SERS-ready" secreted virulence factor due to its strong chromophoric phenazine ring system. No other phenazine (pyoverdine, 1-hydroxyphenazine) has comparable SERS literature.

2. Purine Metabolites — Adenine, Hypoxanthine, Guanine, Xanthine
These are central to the label-free SERS bacterial identification paradigm. They're not virulence factors per se but are secreted degradation products of purine nucleotides released under stress conditions (stringent response).

Fraiman & Ziegler (2024, Talanta) provided the clearest mechanistic explanation: bacterial SERS spectra arise from secreted purine nucleotide degradation products (principally adenine, guanine, xanthine, hypoxanthine) resulting from the (p)ppGpp alarmone-mediated stringent response. They achieved MIC determinations in ~1 hour, effective for both Gram-positive and Gram-negative species.
Cheng et al. (2023, Analytical Chemistry) used purine as an internal standard to recalibrate SERS signals for quantifying adenine and hypoxanthine across 5 orders of magnitude. This addressed the critical reproducibility challenge — calibrations remained effective across 64 substrate batches fabricated over 6 months, with results validated against LC-MS.
Jin et al. (2025, Analytical Chemistry) discovered that heating samples to 60°C accelerates purine metabolite generation, yielding robust SERS spectra within 1 hour instead of >10 hours. ML models achieved 100% accuracy for pathogen identification from both laboratory and clinical specimens, and >91% accuracy distinguishing drug-resistant strains (MRSA, CR-KP).
Ku et al. (2025, Biosensors & Bioelectronics) developed an air-liquid microfluidics-integrated SERS system exploiting "sequential molecular adsorption" to separate purine derivatives with similar SERS substrate affinity, enabling discrimination of strains with identical taxonomy but different antibiotic resistance.
Ghazalová et al. (2025, Sensors) confirmed adenine signals originate from metabolites released into the surrounding environment rather than from the bacterial cell wall, using Au and Ag nanoparticles under osmotic stress conditions across five bacterial strains.
3. Secreted Enzymes — Micrococcal Nuclease (MNase)

Huang et al. (2024, J. Pharmaceutical Analysis) is the only study I found combining SERS with a secreted enzyme target: they detected S. aureus-specific MNase using an Au@AgPt nanozyme array with a cascade triggering strategy. LOD was 6 CFU/mL in SERS mode, tested in serum from sepsis patients (n=7) and healthy controls (n=3), plus longitudinal prognostic monitoring (n=2). This is the closest to a clinically validated secreted-enzyme SERS approach.
Garcia Gonzalez & Hernandez (2022, Expert Review of Molecular Diagnostics) reviewed nuclease activity as a diagnostic biomarker across 15 bacterial pathogens, noting nuclease activity patterns offer advantages over established biomarkers — though this review covers nucleic acid-activatable reporters broadly, not specifically SERS platforms.
4. Siderophores — Enterobactin

Canciu et al. (2022, IJMS) detected enterobactin electrochemically (not SERS) as an E. coli/Gram-negative marker, including multiplex detection alongside PYO for P. aeruginosa. I did not find a dedicated SERS study for enterobactin detection, though Kumar et al. (2024) from the prior search included enterobactin as one of six bacterial biomarkers classified by SERS + CNN on silver nanorod arrays with 99.99% classification accuracy.
Pyoverdine: No SERS detection papers surfaced. Kumar et al. (2022, JBC) developed fluorescent sensors for siderophores (enterobactin, pyoverdine, aerobactin, yersiniabactin) using bacterial membrane receptor proteins — a complementary but non-SERS approach.
5. Other Secreted Metabolites

Indole (E. coli signaling molecule): Jayan et al. (2022, Applied Spectroscopy) detected indole from E. coli cultures using Au@Ag core-shell NPs at 0.0886 mM LOD in LB media. Vali et al. (2025, bioRxiv) achieved 100 nM LOD for indole using nanoplasmonic SERS — over 500-fold more sensitive than prior methods — and identified a novel TnaA-dependent metabolite in uropathogenic E. coli.
Calcium dipicolinate (CaDPA) from bacterial spores: Segervald et al. (2025, ACS Sensors) detected Bacillus spores at 10³ spores/mL via CaDPA released by probe sonication, using gold nanorods.
6. Quorum Sensing Molecules (AHLs)
This is a notable gap. The Liu et al. SERS chip mentioned above monitors PYO as a QS signal molecule, but I found no SERS study specifically targeting acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs) for infection detection. Frigoli et al. (2024, ACS Omega) detected N-butyryl homoserine lactone (BHL) from P. aeruginosa using molecularly imprinted polymer electrochemistry (LOD 31.78 nM) — but electrochemical, not SERS. AHLs lack the strong chromophoric groups that make phenazines so SERS-amenable, which likely explains the gap.

Molecules with the strongest Raman-active functional groups for SERS (from what I found): pyocyanin's phenazine ring system, purine bases (adenine, hypoxanthine) with their N-heterocyclic ring breathing modes, and indole's aromatic system all show excellent SERS enhancement. Siderophores with catechol groups (enterobactin) should theoretically enhance well due to metal-catechol charge transfer, but this hasn't been systematically exploited.
