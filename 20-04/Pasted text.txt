Rapid Raman Fingerprinting Identification of Listeria monocytogenes via Immunomagnetic Enrichment Coupled with Droplet-Shrinkage-Assisted SERS
Hui He †,‡, Panyu Liang †,‡, Yutong Lin †,‡, Xiaohan Li †,‡, Duoduo Xue †,‡, Meihong Wu †,‡, Fei Zhao †,‡, Keping Ye †,‡, Chunbao Li †,‡, Guanghong Zhou †,‡, Changbo Tang †,‡,*

Abstract
Rapid identification of Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes) is critical to preventing outbreaks of foodborne listeriosis. Conventional methods required immunoassay or nucleic acid sequence recognition for indirect/labeled detection or identification of L. monocytogenes, separately. Herein, a facile label-free fingerprinting method was developed for direct identification of L. monocytogenes using immunomagnetic enrichment coupled with droplet-shrinkage-assisted surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS). Immunomagnetic beads were used for specific enrichment of L. monocytogenes from the complex matrix. Silver nanoparticles were shrunk to bacterial cell surface, which excited bacterial Raman fingerprints under radiation. The enhanced Raman signal at 477 cm–1 was selected for quantification, and the limit of detection was 7 CFU/mL in real meat sample juice. The consistent results between the proposed method and the plate counting method demonstrated its applicability. High accuracy, speed, and sensitivity endowed this method with a great potential for monitoring L. monocytogenes contamination in meat and meat products.

Introduction
Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes) is of great health concern for the food industry because it is characterized by a high hospitalization rate of 96%. Due to the severity of listeriosis, especially for high-risk groups, including pregnant women, neonates, the elderly, and immunodeficient people, the existing level of L. monocytogenes in foods must be kept minimal to ensure product safety. L. monocytogenes can grow in various meat and meat products, mainly including ready-to-eat meat and poultry products. , Owing to inhibitory growth of microbe, refrigeration is usually applied for meat and meat products storage, however, as a psychrophile, L. monocytogenes might survive under low temperature. Besides, for ready-to-eat poultry products, huge consumption and storage make it difficult to strictly control microbial contamination during food chain. According to the EFSA report of 2022, the reported listeriosis cases were 2738 in total and up to 286 deaths in the EU, showing that listeriosis is still one of the most serious foodborne diseases under EU surveillance. To control such a public health problem, a more efficient monitoring program is necessary. Therefore, simple, rapid, and sensitive monitoring L. monocytogenes in poultry meats is required.

Among the various methods which are available for either counting and/or detecting of L. monocytogenes, colony plate counting method has been widely considered as the gold-standard method for L. monocytogenes detection. However, this approach is time-consuming and provides a retrospective outcome. To overcome these problems, several methods have been implemented, e.g., quantitative estimation of kinetic parameters related to growth, survival, and death of this pathogen or rapid detection techniques mainly including immunoassay and nucleic acid analysis. Immunoassay has been developed as a powerful technique for L. monocytogenes detection due to the high specificity of monoclonal antibodies toward surface antigens of bacterial cell. An improved enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was developed for L. monocytogenes determination with catalase-mediated gold nanoparticle growth as colorimetric signal, exhibiting a low limit of detection (LOD) of 80 CFU/g lower than conventional ELISA by at least 2 orders of magnitude. For on-site detection, lateral flow immunoassays (LFA) have been explored. Though conventional LFA suffered from low sensitivity and reproducibility. Advanced LFA techniques, such as crystal violet absorption on bacterial cell or “end-on” orientation of antibody on gold nanoparticles, perovskite quantum dots-based chemiluminescence, and photothermal lysis-induced bioluminescence, have achieved ultrahigh sensitivity with the LOD less than 10 CFU/mL. Nevertheless, immunoassay always suffered from false-positive detection. Nucleic acid sequence-based molecular detection techniques offer higher accuracy and sensitivity due to gene-level identification and amplification. These methods require bacterial lysis and sequence amplification, such as DNA hybridization for capturing genes like hlyA, detected via magnetic nanoparticles or fullerol for electrochemical measurements. Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) system emerged for precise gene capture and amplification, including LFA, electrochemistry, and fluorescence. However, bacterial lysis and sequence amplification were time-consuming, cumbersome, and made it difficult to distinguish viable or dead cells. Therefore, an alternative method with speed and identification capability remains to be explored.

Surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) is receiving significant interest owing to its higher detection sensitivity and diverse detection methods (Laing et al., 2017). SERS depends on the dual amplification of chemical interactions and electromagnetic fields surrounding metallic substrates to significantly amplify the Raman signals of target analytes bound to the substrates. , Frequently employed SERS substrates consist of noble metals such as silver, gold, and copper nanoparticles. , In recent years, owing to their high sensitivity, SERS-related techniques have been extensively employed in microbiological research. , SERS is emphasized as an exceptionally sensitive method that yields molecular “fingerprints” for the identification of pathogens. , To our knowledge, only one study has documented Raman fingerprints of L. monocytogenes utilizing gold nanocomposites as a SERS substrate; however, the potential of SERS fingerprinting has not been explored for complex food or meat matrices.

Here, we report a rapid Raman fingerprinting method for quantitative detection and identification of L. monocytogenes via combining the merits of immunomagnetic enrichment (IME) and droplet-shrinkage assisted label-free (DS-LF) SERS fingerprinting. Immunomagnetic separation is employed for effectively capturing bacteria and avoiding the interference of food matrices. Droplet shrinkage might provide an effective and facile coagulation of bacteria and nanoparticles. Therefore, we aim to accomplish a continuous two-step enrichment and fingerprinting of L. monocytogenes via immunomagnetic separation and droplet-shrinkage induced coagulation, meanwhile, the concentrated complex can be exploited to directly excite the Raman fingerprints of L. monocytogenes and realize label-free SERS identification and quantification. By coupling biotin anti-L. monocytogenes antibody with streptavidin-modified magnetic beads, and the resulting immunomagnetic beads were used for the primary enrichment of bacteria. With noble metal nanoparticles as localized Raman signal actuators in fusion with bacteria, the complex of nanoparticles and bacteria is condensed during colloidal droplet shrinkage after air drying for secondary enrichment of Raman actuators. The nanoparticle-conjugated biomolecules in target bacteria can be excited out of inherent Raman spectra that can be considered as fingerprints of L. monocytogenes. The method of IME-DS-LF-SERS is established under the optimized conditions. The proposed method is successfully applied in real chicken sample analysis and showed reduced detection time, high detection sensitivity, and satisfied accuracy compared with the classical plate counting method. Therefore, the proposed method has great potential for rapid microbial identification and quantification in meat safety control.

Experimental Section
Materials and Reagents
Trisodium citrate, silver nitrate (AgNO3), chloroauric acid (HAuCl4), and phosphate buffered saline with Tween-20 (PBST) were purchased from Sino Biopharmaceutical (Shanghai, China). Streptavidin magnetic beads were purchased from MCE (Shanghai, China). Biotinylated anti-L. monocytogenes antibody was purchased from Abcam (Shanghai, China). L. monocytogenes, Salmonella typhimurium (S. typhimurium), and Escherichia coli (E. coli) bacteria cultures were stored in our laboratory. These bacteria were subcultured twice at 37 °C. 5 mL portion of bacteria solution was harvested and washed twice with ultrapure water by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 2 min. Bacteria colonies were diluted 10-fold and counted after incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the obtained bacteria were utilized for the SERS measurement.

Instrumentation
Absorbance measurements and UV–vis spectra were collected by a UV–vis spectrophotometer (MD, M2e, USA). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained in a Regulus-8100 instrument (Hitach, Japan). Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images were collected on a HT-770 (Hitachi, Japan). Particle sizes were measured in a laser particle size analyzer (Malvern, Mastersizer nano ZS90, U.K.). Microscopic images were obtained with a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, TSC SP8 X, Germany). Raman measurements were performed using a microscopy confocal laser Raman spectrometer (Horiba, LabRAM HR Evolution, France). In this study, the tested sample was first deposited on an aluminum sheet after the droplet naturally evaporated. Then, the Raman spectra were measured on the Raman system under radiation of a 785 nm laser at 25% laser power and 5s acquisition time. Recorded Raman signal was in the range of 300–1700 cm–1. 20 spectra were acquired at constant conditions for each sample at room temperature.

Synthesis and Characterization of Colloidal Metal Nanoparticles and Their Absorption on Bacteria
Synthesis of Silver Nanoparticles (AgNPs)
AgNPs were synthesized by the citrate reduction method (Scheme S1). Briefly, AgNO3 was solved by deionized water with the final concentration of 5 mM. When the solution was boiled, 3 mL of 1% (w/w) trisodium citrate solution was added immediately into 60 mL of the prepared AgNO3 solution and the mixture was kept boiling for 30 min. Finally, AgNPs solution was obtained and stored at 4 °C in the refrigerator for use.

Synthesis of Gold Nanoparticles (AuNPs)
AuNPs were synthesized by the citrate reduction method (Scheme S2). Briefly, 950 μL of a 5 mg/mL HAuCl4 solution was mixed with 60 mL of pure water and then boiled under magnetic stirring. Subsquently, 500 μL of 1% (w/w) trisodium citrate solution was added and the mixture was kept boiling for 30 min. Finally, AuNPs solution was obtained and stored at 4 °C in the refrigerator for use.

Synthesis of Gold Core-Silver Shell Nanoparticles (Au@Ag NPs)
Au@Ag NPs were prepared by a seed-mediated growth method (Scheme S3). Employing 3 mL of the prepared AuNPs as the seeds, 300 μL of 10 mM ascorbic acid solution as reductant, and 300 μL of 10 mM AgNO3 as growth solution, the Au@Ag NPs were formed under gentle stirring for 10 min. Finally, Au@Ag NPs solution was obtained and stored at 4 °C in the refrigerator for use.

Characterization of Nanoparticle Absorption on Bacteria
Various nanoparticles, including AgNPs, AuNPs, and Au@Ag NPs, were mixed with a L. monocytogenes bacterial suspension (107 CFU/mL), separately. Dropping 5 μL of bacterial suspension on a piece of tinfoil and 5 μL of nanoparticle colloid was subsequently added dropwise. The droplet-fusion procedure was repeated 3 to 5 times by a pipet. The droplet-shrinkage was performed on a microcomputer-controlled heating plate at the temperature of 30 °C under a blowing rate of 0.4 m/s, and the shrunk droplet containing the bacteria-nanoparticle complex was transferred onto a copper mesh for TEM observation after removal of the suspended components.

Finite-Difference Time-Domain Simulation
For nanoparticle systems consisting of individual or a few closely coupled particles (AgNPs, AuNPs, or Au@AgNPs), the electric field distribution surrounding the nanoparticle(s) was simulated directly using the FDTD method. To model the electric field distribution for a real ensemble of nanoparticles under experimental conditions, the overall field within a representative simulation area containing a finite number of nanoparticles was calculated. This basic simulation area was scaled to a certain area of 150 × 150 nm2 that was a typical plasmonic enhancement unit containing enough nanoparticle assemblies. In each case, the spatial arrangement of nanoparticles within the simulation area was generated with the aid of TEM observation, and the resulting coordinates served as input for the FDTD simulation.

SERS Detection Condition Optimization
Effect of Bacterial Resuspension Solutions
The precipitate of L. monocytogenes (107 CFU/mL) after centrifugal washing was resuspended with saline, PBS, and ultrapure water, respectively, and 2 μL of each resuspended bacterial solution was sucked up, 2 μL of AgNPs was added dropwise on a piece of tinfoil, and the droplet-fusion procedure was repeated 3–5 times. The resulting droplet was gradually shrunken on the ultraclean bench to dry at 20 °C under blowing rate of 0.4 m/s, and finally tested by the SERS spectra.

Effect of Concentration of AgNPs
The prepared AgNPs solutions were concentrated to 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 times, separately, and the optimal AgNPs concentration was dropped on the L. monocytogenes target spot (107 CFU/mL). Under mild air flow, the droplet was evaporated, and the AgNPs were deposited on the surface of target bacteria.

Sensitivity of DS-LF-SERS Fingerprinting Method
Raman spectra of different concentrations (102–107 CFU/mL) of L. monocytogenes were measured using the optimized acquisition conditions with the laser wavelength at 785 nm and laser power at 25% under 5 s of acquisition time.

Preparation of Immunomagnetic Beads
IMBs were prepared by a two-step functionalization (Scheme S4). 100 μL streptavidin magnetic beads solutions were washed with 1 mL buffer I (PBST containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin) for 2× times. Afterward, 5 μL of biotin anti-L. monocytogenes antibody was immediately added into 1 mL of the solution by thorough mixing and then placed on the rotator at 900 rpm at room temperature for 1.5 h. After magnetic separation, to block the unconjugated sites of IMBs, the complexes were washed with 1 mL buffer I for 2× times. Subsequently the IMBs were resuspended in 1 mL buffer I and stored at 4 °C.

IME Condition Optimization
Immune Capture Time
IMBs was added into 300 μL of PBS containing 107 CFU of L. monocytogenes and mixed by a rotator with 900 rpm at different immunoreaction time (0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 min). The bead-bacterium complexes were concentrated on the side of the tube by a magnetic separator for 2 min. Absorbance measurements of 200 μL of supernatant were obtained with an UV–visible spectrophotometer. The absorbance difference between before and after enrichment was used for evaluating the capture efficiency.

Amount of IMBs for Immune Capture
Different volumes of IMBs (10, 20, 30, and 40 μL) were added to 300 μL of PBS containing 107 CFU/m L. monocytogenes and mixed by a rotator at 900 rpm at 40 min. The bead-bacteria complexes were concentrated on the side of the tube by a magnetic separator for 2 min. Absorbance measurements of 200 μL of supernatant were obtained with an UV–vis spectrophotometer. The absorbance difference between before and after enrichment was used for evaluating the capture efficiency.

Characterization of Bacteria Captured on IMBs by SEM
1 mL portion of PBS containing 107 CFU/mL of L. monocytogenes was mixed with streptavidin magnetic beads with Biotin anti-L. monocytogenes antibody and streptavidin magnetic beads without Biotin anti-L. monocytogenes antibody by a rotator at 900 rpm for 40 min, respectively. The magnetic bead-bacteria complexes were captured on the side of the tube by a magnetic separator for 2 min. The collected composites were scanned with SEM to characterize the IMBs-bacteria complexes.

Specificity Test
The specificity of the IMBs was evaluated using nontarget bacteria, such as E. coli, S. typhimurium, L. monocytogenes, E. coli, which were cultured in BHI at 37 °C for 12 h, the bacteria were not mixing and, respectively, captured by the IMBs to validate the specificity of the method. After magnetic separation, 200 μL of supernatant were obtained and determined with an UV–visible spectrophotometer.

Detection of L. monocytogenes by IME-DS-LF-SERS Fingerprinting Method
IMS-SERS was constructed by a combination of SERS and immunomagnetic separation. Briefly, the IMBs and the sample solution containing different concentrations of L. monocytogenes (10–107 CFU/mL) were mixed and incubated at 25 °C for 40 min. The solution was isolated under magnetic force and redispersed in 10 μL of PBS (10 mM, pH 7.4). Then 2 μL of AgNPs were added to the 2 μL the IMBs-bacteria complexes and dried to Raman test. The SERS spectra corresponding to L. monocytogenes were collected. The calibration curve was constructed by obtaining the average SERS reading of L. monocytogenes (10–107 CFU/mL).

Real Application of IME-DS-LF-SERS in Chicken Sample
L. monocytogenes prepared in different concentrations (104–106 CFU/mL) was added to sterilized chicken samples. After thoroughly mixing, the IMBs were added to the sample supernatant and incubated for 40 min. Then the IME-DS-LF-SERS method was conducted to detect the L. monocytogenes in the supernatant. The results were compared to the traditional plate counting method.

Statistical Analysis
Baseline correction and data graphing were performed using LabSpec 6.0 (Horiba) and OriginLab (Edu. ver) software. The spectral data were preprocessed for linear baseline correction to avoid any variables shifting above the baseline and to ensure that all SERS spectra were obtained with the same baseline. To ensure data stability and reliability, all data measurements were repeated at least three times.

Results and Discussion
Principle of IME-DS-LF-SERS Fingerprinting of L. monocytogenes
In order to realize simultaneously ultrasensitive identification and quantification of L. monocytogenes, we designed a targeted SERS fingerprinting method as shown in Figure . When mixing bacteria and plasmonic nanoparticles into one droplet, solvent evaporation-induced droplet shrinkage promoted the coagulation of microscale bacteria and nanoscale plasmonic metal substrate (Figure A). Owing to the interaction between bacterial cell surface components and plasmonic metal nanoparticles (Figure B), the Raman fingerprints of these components were excited under laser radiation (Figure C). For LF-SERS detection, the plasmonic metal nanoparticles were synthesized by citrate reduction methods or ascorbic acid mediated seed growth method, thus these nanoparticles were negatively charged. On nanoparticle surface, the negatively charged ligands-coated region might facilitate nanoparticles to poke negatively charged N-acetylmuramic acid layer and the other bare region could bind with proteins through multiple interactions between cell surface peptides/proteins and metal nanoparticles. − L. monocytogenes targeted immunomagnetic beads were prepared through surface modification on streptavidin functionalized magnetic beads. To reduce abundant biomolecule interference in the meat matrix, BSA was first modified on such beads owing to its antifouling properties. Then, biotin conjugated anti-L. monocytogenes antibodies were bonded on magnetic beads through interaction between biotin and streptavidin (Figure D). The prepared immunomagnetic beads (IMBs) could capture the target L. monocytogenes regardless of the rich protein or other molecules in meat juice. Once the L. monocytogenes were specifically captured by immunomagnetic beads, the interferences could be washed out and separated from target bacteria. After mixing with numerous metal nanoparticles, the concentrated L. monocytogenes could adsorb metal nanoparticles from colloidal suspension and for NP-protein composites on the bacteria surface during the droplet-shrinkage process. Under laser radiation, the Raman fingerprints of bacterial cell surface components were excited by the SERS effect and collected by a Raman spectrometer, which could reflect the diagnostic Raman spectra for detection and identification of L. monocytogenes. The complete detection workflow was illustrated in Figure E. The proposed method mainly includes a two-step continuous concentration procedure for target bacteria enrichment and a subsequent label-free SERS detection procedure for fingerprinting L. monocytogenes. Immunomagnetic separation facilitated targe bacterial enrichment, and subsequently, droplet-shrinkage-assisted bacteria-nanoparticle coagulation promoted nanoparticle adsorption on the bacterial cell surface. Both concentration procedures were favorable for ultrasensitive identification and quantification of L. monocytogenes via SERS fingerprinting.

Droplet-Shrinkage-Assisted Label-Free SERS Fingerprinting of L. monocytogenes
Preparation and Characterization of Plasmonic Metal Nanoparticles
To achieve label-free SERS fingerprinting of L. monocytogenes, we synthesized three plasmonic nanoscale substrates for direct adsorption onto target bacteria surface via droplet-shrinkage assisted coagulation of bacteria and nanoparticles. The three nanoscale noble metal substrates, namely AgNPs, AuNPs, and Au@AgNPs, were prepared by chemical reduction method and capped with negative charged ligands, such as citrate and ascorbic acids, and the corresponding images are shown in Figure A. For certain nanoparticles, both negatively charged region and bare region covered the nanoparticle surface, among which negatively charged ligands might aid to poke the peptidoglycan shell and facilitate bare region to bond the cell surface components , (other details see Effect of Metal Nanoparticle Substrates on SERS Fingerprinting). The morphology of these nanoparticles was characterized by TEM, showing that spherical particles were successfully synthesized (Figure B). The UV–visible absorption spectra of these substrates are depicted in Figure C. The prepared noble metal nanoparticles exhibited distinct absorption peaks at approximately 410 and 520 nm, 520 nm, and both of them, separately, further indicating that the three plasmonic nanoparticles were successfully synthesized and could be used as SERS fingerprinting through plasmonic effect under irradiation of laser wavelength at 785 nm. The particle size of these nanoscale SERS substrates was also confirmed by DLS (Figure D). The average sizes of AgNPs, AuNPs, and Au@AgNPs were evaluated as 21.8 ± 2.1, 24.7 ± 0.7, and 48.5 ± 1.7 nm. The larger size of Au@AgNPs was due to Ag shell growth on the AuNPs seeds. All of these nanoparticles could be exploited as the plasmonic nanoscale substrate to enhance the Raman fingerprint signal of target bacteria.

Characterization and Simulation of Multiple Complexes of Nanoparticles and Bacteria
To clearly exhibit the adsorption performance of these plasmonic nanoparticle bacteria onto the bacteria after droplet-shrinkage, we collected the complex of nanoparticles and bacteria by controlling the droplet-shrinkage process on a temperature-controlled heating plate. The resulting complex of nanoparticles and bacteria was characterized under TEM as shown in Figure , revealing that droplet shrinkage drives obvious adsorption of the tested nanoparticles (AgNPs, AuNPs, and Au@AgNPs) onto the bacterial surface. Notably, compared with AuNPs and Au@AgNPs, AgNPs exhibited more tight and high-density adsorption behavior, especially in multiple nanoparticle assembly between bacterial cells, whereas AuNPs and Au@AgNPs also could adsorb on the bacteria, but the distribution is relatively dispersed. The compact assembled nanoparticles could provide more intense electromagnetic field enhancement, , thus we further calculated the near field enhancement through finite difference time domain (FDTD) method. The nanoparticle models were constructed according to several typical nanoparticle assemblies frequently observed in the TEM images. The results demonstrated that a more compact assembly induced more intense near field enhancement and produced more plasmonic hot spots for subsequent Raman signal amplification. This confirmed that droplet confinement effectively increased plasmonic hot spots in the bacterial cell interface, which is favorable for Raman signal enhancement of bacterial surface components.

Effect of Metal Nanoparticle Substrates on SERS Fingerprinting
The plasmonic nanoscale substrates could contact with surface components of target bacteria to excite the inherent Raman scattering of bacteria selves, especially bacterial exterior surface proteins and polysaccharides, including peptidoglycan, internalins (InlA, InlB, etc.), amidase (Ami), and actin assembly inducing protein (ActA). When mixing these plasmonic substrates with L. monocytogenes through droplet fusion, SERS fingerprints of L. monocytogenes were obtained as shown in Figure A–C. Among these substrates, AgNPs exhibited the highest Raman signal enhancement effect for all of the observed Raman peaks, especially at 477, 658, 730, 966, 1134, 1229, 1314, 1445, 1578, and 1640 cm–1, while these peaks were proven to be not from AgNPs (Figure S1). Given that the SERS substrates were excessive to bacteria, the higher Raman signal enhancement by AgNPs compared with AuNPs or Au@AgNPs might be related to higher affinity of AgNPs toward bacterial wall of L. monocytogenes. All of these strong peaks were considered as characteristic features of L. monocytogenes. The peak at 477 cm–1 might be associated with glycosidic bond in peptidoglycan. The peak at 730 cm–1 should be assigned to the glycan ring in peptidoglycan (Figure D). Both 477 and 730 cm–1 were almost single featured peaks and glycan-related ones, while the other peaks were overlapped with each other and assigned to peptide/protein according to previous reports. − Given the relatively thick cell wall of L. monocytogenes up to ca. 30 nm (Figure B), inner components in cell membrane and cytoplasm could not be excited under plasmonic effect of these nanoscale substrates, , while the cell wall proteins could mainly be responsible for the observed Raman fingerprints. The typical secondary structures like α-helix and β-sheet were obviously observed in the Raman fingerprints such as the peaks at 1229, 1314, and 1640 cm–1. In addition, three Raman active amino acid residues, namely tyrosine (Tyr), tryptophan (Trp), and phenylalanine (Phe), might contribute to the Raman fingerprint. − The Raman peaks at 658, 966, 1045, and 1578 cm–1 were assigned to the three amino acids, and the corresponding residues are compared in Figure E. Notably, for various bacteria, Raman fingerprints have been proved to be derived from several metabolites, especially purines and their derivatives. − These findings are crucial for deciphering and interpreting the Raman spectra of bacteria because the purine salvage pathway plays an important role in its adaptation to nutrient-limited environments, including food matrices and intracellular niches. Genomic analyses show that many bacteria encode canonical salvage enzymes such as hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase, adenine phosphoribosyltransferase, and purine nucleoside phosphorylases, together with transporters that import extracellular purine bases and nucleosides. However, for L. monocytogenes, detailed studies on the purine salvage pathway remain insufficient to confirm purine metabolites as the source of its Raman fingerprint spectrum. Thus, the accurate Raman peaks assignment remains to be explored in the future.

Characterization of Droplet-Shrinkage Process
To elucidate the coagulation of bacteria and nanoparticles, we assumed the droplet-shrinkage process as shown in Figure A, and we tried to characterize this process by confocal imaging and particle size distribution measuring. In a comparison of starting and ending stages during droplet evaporation, the dispersive and separated bacterial cells and nanoparticles tended to be complexly induced by droplet-shrinkage, which had been confirmed by obvious shadowiness around most bacteria after droplet-shrinkage (Figure B). To clarify the unique droplet-shrinkage process, we directly observed the adsorption process under Raman microscopy, as shown in Figures S2–S4. The results suggested that many nanoparticles were adsorbed on the surface of the bacteria, in favorable for following Raman signal excitation and fingerprinting. Though the bacteria bonded numerous nanoparticles, the composite size distribution range slightly increased from 1106–2304 nm to 1281–2669 nm (Figure C), indicating that the composite remained dispersive and the nanoparticles had limited effect on the integrity and aggregation of bacteria in such short droplet shrinking duration.

Effect of Droplet Resuspension Solutions on SERS Fingerprinting
The nanoparticle resuspension solution might affect the absorption behavior of the SERS nanoscale substrates with bacterial cells during droplet-shrinkage. To investigate the effect of solvent on SERS fingerprinting, we compared three resuspension solutions, including ultrapure water, PBS, and saline. As illustrated in Figure D, ultrapure water is more favorable for Raman signal enhancement than PBS and saline. As ionic strength of resuspension solution increased, the SERS signal gradually decreased. This discrepancy might be related to the distinct binding states of the AgNPs and bacteria. Given that both thick peptidoglycan encapsulated bacteria and citrate capped AgNPs were negatively charged, the main interaction between them was presumed to the bonding of bacterial cell surface protein and nanoparticles. In the case of physiological saline and PBS buffer, the weaker SERS signals could be attributed to the unstable dispersion of AgNPs in solution. Especially, NaCl would disrupt the surface charge balance of the particles, weakening electrostatic repulsion between particles and promoting aggregation. , This aggregation might limit movement of nanoparticles toward the bacterial surface, thus reducing the overall bonding amounts of AgNPs on the bacterial and finally diminishing the SERS enhancement effect. Conversely, ultrapure water in the absence of interfering ions prevented SERS substrate aggregation, enabling more SERS substrates to accumulate onto the bacterial surface and resulting in higher SERS fingerprint signals.

Effect of AgNPs Concentration in Droplet on SERS Fingerprinting
Nanoparticle quantity in the droplet might also influence the final adsorption behavior of nanoparticles on the bacterial cell surface. To investigate effect of AgNPs concentrations on SERS fingerprinting, different concentrations (2.5, 5, 10, and 20 mM) of AgNPs are compared in Figure E. It was clear that the concentration of AgNPs significantly affected the Raman signal intensity. As the AgNPs concentration increased, the Raman intensity of L. monocytogenes initially rose to the highest signal in the concentration of 10 mM and then declined in spite of using more AgNPs. The improved signal was attributed to the increasing number of AgNPs in the resuspension solution, promoting more AgNPs tending to the cell surface of L. monocytogenes and generating more plasmonic hotspots on the bacterial wall for Raman fingerprint signal amplification. However, additional AgNPs caused a decrease of SERS signal, which could be related to the fact that more stabilizing agent, namely citrate ions, increased the ionic strength as described above and brought about nanoparticle aggregation rather than adsorption on the bacteria. Therefore, adequate but appropriate usage of SERS substrate was necessary for maximizing SERS fingerprint signal.

Sensitivity Test
Detection sensitivity was the most important factor in evaluating the proposed method. A set of different concentrations of L. monocytogenes were tested to investigate the sensitivity. As shown in Figures F and S5, the Raman fingerprint signals of L. monocytogenes were still clearly observed with the bacterial concentration of 102 CFU/mL and gradually became more intense as the concentration of target bacteria increased. By fitting the linear relationship presented between the Raman signal intensity of different (102 CFU/mL–107 CFU/mL) concentrations and the logarithm of 10 of its concentration, the Raman fingerprint signal intensity at the Raman shift of 1332 cm–1 showed a regular enhancement. The linear regression equation was y = 1200.9x – 1671.9, R 2 = 0.9895, and the linear range and the limit of detection (LOD) was calculated as 102 CFU/mL–107 CFU/mL and 30 CFU/mL, separately, showing an outstanding detection sensitivity for label-free SERS fingerprinting.

Immunomagnetic Enrichment of L. monocytogenes
To accurately capture the target L. monocytogenes in the complex meat juice matrix, we prepared highly specific immunomagnetic beads (IMBs) through antibody conjugation by biotin–streptavidin interaction and blocking by bovine serum albumin modification (Scheme S4). It is evident that the streptavidin-magnetic bead (Fe3O4@SA) size is ca. 983 nm, and after bonding with antibodies, the size of IMBs (Fe3O4@SA@Ab) was increased to ca. 1003 nm (Figure A). The larger particle size of the immunomagnetic beads demonstrated successful connection of biotin modified anti-L. monocytogenes immunoglobulin to streptavidin functionalized magnetic beads. The Raman spectra of the IMBs showed that they only had one negligible peak, which would not affect subsequent Raman detection (Figure S6). Thus, the prepared IMBs could be employed for capturing L. monocytogenes.

The amount of IMBs determined the total bacterial capture efficiency (CE) owing to the total binding sites provided by IMBs. Therefore, it was crucial to optimize the number of IMBs. As shown in Figure B, with the quantities of IMBs varying from 10 to 40 μg, the absorbance of collected bacteria first gently increased to 0.018 and then slightly decreased. The results illustrated that the maximum capture amount required at least 30 μg of IMBs for 300 μL of 107 CFU/mL L. monocytogenes. Excessive IMBs were not necessary because bead collision might be prone to destruction and result in a capture efficiency decrease. Consequently, 30 μg of IMBs was chosen as the optimal amount for IME. Following incubation with the bacterial suspension for 40 min, the CE reached maximum and remained stable, which was also confirmed by plate counting method (Figure S7).

Immunorecognition time was another factor that affected capture efficiency. To further improve the concentration efficiency of IMBs, the immunoreaction time was carefully optimized in the range of 0 to 50 min (Figure C). Employing 300 μL of a bacterial suspension containing 107 CFU/mL of L. monocytogenes as the simulated sample, the absorbance of captured bacteria exhibited a slight improvement under less than 40 min of incubation. However, when immunoreaction time continued to increase to 40 min, the absorbance dramatically reached at the maximum point and remained constant in spite of prolonging incubation time. Therefore, to realize optimal enrichment efficiency while minimizing the overall processing time, the optimal immunoreaction time was selected as 40 min.

To directly observe whether IMBs could bind bacterial cells, the complex of magnetic beads and bacteria were characterized under SEM. Figure D shows that only when antibody was conjugated to the magnetic beads, the target L. monocytogenes could be bonded on the bead surface, otherwise most bacteria cells were isolated from the beads. It was noted that the sizes of beads and bacteria were comparable, but high affinity between bacteria and IMBs led to closely packed particle clusters, while such phenomenon could not be observed in the control.

To assess the specificity of IMBs, we tested six pathogens including various L. monocytogenes (L.m 19,112, L.m 19,115, L.m 19,117, and L.m 15,313), E. coli (E. coli), and S. typhimurium (S. typhimurium). As shown in Figure E, the capture efficiency for E. coli and S. typhimurium was much lower than that of L. monocytogenes, demonstrating that the prepared IMBs exhibit high specificity toward L. monocytogenes strains and high anti-interference ability. Meanwhile, it was expected that this method would be suitable for multiple Listeria monocytogene strains. Consequently, the prepared IMBs had great potential in coupling with DS-LF-SERS fingerprinting for rapid identification of L. monocytogene.

To clarify the unique adsorption process of droplet-shrinkage on the IMB-bacteria complex, we performed a comparative study between our droplet-shrinkage method and the conventional nanoparticle incubation method. Before testing, we first confirmed that both IMB and the complex of IMB and AgNPs exhibited relatively clean background in the Raman spectra (Figures S6–S8). The microscopic adsorption between nanoparticles and the IMB-bacteria complex was characterized via SEM (Figure S9), and Raman signal reproducibility was also investigated (Figure S10). In conventional magnetic separation method, when captured bacteria-magnetic bead complexes were incubated with a colloidal AgNP solution followed by magnetic separation, nanoparticle adsorption onto the bacterial surface is inherently sparse and stochastic. This resulted in inconsistent and often insufficient nanoparticle coverage, leading to highly variable SERS signal intensity from point to point. In stark contrast, our approach employed a controlled evaporation process. By applying a droplet containing the target complex and AgNPs and allowing it to shrink, we actively promoted the physical compression and enrichment of nanoparticles directly onto the surface of the magnetically immobilized bacteria. This created a dense, conformal layer of AgNPs around each target cell, ensuring a high and reproducible local concentration of nanoparticles and, consequently, a high density of interparticle plasmonic “hot spots” adjacent to the bacterial cell wall for stable signal amplification. In addition, we also evaluated the laser spot size in our instrument, and the large spot size of ca. 10 μm ensured that the acquired SERS spectrum represents an ensemble average from multiple L. monocytogenes cells within the laser spot. This averaging effect was essential to obtaining a reproducible and representative bacterial fingerprint, mitigating signal variations that might arise from the precise nanoscale distribution of nanoparticles on any single cell.

IME-DS-LF-SERS Detection of L. monocytogenes in Real Chicken Samples
Chicken or other poultry meats have consistently faced the threat of L. monocytogenes in which various matrix interference could pose challenges for the bacterial detection, thus we optimized this method with chicken meat as a proof-of-concept. Under the optimal capture and detection conditions of the proposed IME-DS-LF-SERS fingerprinting method, we validated the analysis performance including sensitivity, reproducibility, and linear range before application in real chicken samples. A series of gradient concentrations of L. monocytogenes were employed as the simulated samples. Through linear fitting, we establish a standard curve for the integrated IME-DS-LF-SERS method specifically for fingerprinting L. monocytogenes. As presented in Figure F,G, the Raman signal exhibited a linear increase with rising target bacterial concentration, demonstrating the capability of this approach for quantitative detection. A satisfied linear correlation (y = 272.13x – 290.61, R 2 = 0.9949) was obtained between the intensity of the SERS signal and the logarithm of the concentration of L. monocytogenes, which was also validated using the standardized plate count method. This correlation was established within a concentration range of 10 to 107 CFU/mL, with a remarkable LOD of 7 CFU/mL. The practical applicability of the IME-DS-LF-SERS method for detecting L. monocytogenes in real-world samples was investigated using chicken meat purchased from a local supermarket. To assess the matrix effect in the proposed assay, aqueous extracts from the chicken meat were spiked with varying concentrations of L. monocytogenes (1 × 104–1 × 106 CFU/mL). Meanwhile, a control experimental result using nonspiked clean chicken sample confirmed the absence of this target signal, demonstrating the specificity of the assay in the complex meat juice matrix (Figure S12). The results obtained from the IME-DS-LF-SERS detection were then compared to those of the traditional plate counting method (Figure H), and no significant difference was observed between both methods, demonstrating the excellent accuracy of the proposed method for L. monocytogenes detection. These findings had proven the promising potential and credibility of the IME-DS-LF-SERS fingerprinting approach for reliable detection of L. monocytogenes in chicken samples.

Compared with other associated methods for L. monocytogenes detection (Table S1), the proposed method exhibited several unique advantages, including label-free, direct identification without nucleic acid amplification or antibody labeling, excellent analytical sensitivity (LOD: 7 CFU/mL in real meat juice), and short workflow owing to rapid IME and droplet-shrinkage-driven SERS enhancement. In the recent IME-SERS studies, − labeled SERS mode was selected for indirect detection of L. monocytogenes, however, in our work, the direct detection/identification of the bacterium was achieved by droplet-shrinkage assisted label-free SERS fingerprinting, which has not been studied in the literatures to the best of our knowledge. Besides, other merits also endowed our method with excellent analytical performances as follows. Unlike methods that rely on prefunctionalized SERS tags or the direct mixing of preaggregated nanoparticles with captured bacteria, our approach employed a controlled, postcapture evaporation step. This droplet-shrinkage actively facilitated the formation of a dense, conformal shell of silver nanoparticles exclusively onto the surface of magnetically immobilized bacteria. This creates a highly localized and reproducible enhancement zone precisely where the target cells are located, directly amplifying their intrinsic molecular fingerprints while minimizing background interference from unbound nanoparticles or matrix components. This method also offers a uniquely convenient workflow. Following target capture and magnetic concentration using IMBs, the entire SERS substrate preparation could be achieved in situ. It just required the application of a colloidal silver nanoparticle-containing droplet to the aggregated bacteria-IMB complex, followed by ambient evaporation. This eliminates the need for complex, multistep nanoparticle conjugation chemistry, secondary incubations, or repeated washing steps to remove unbound reporters. The IMBs not only provide specificity and preconcentration but also physically localize the bacteria, ensuring the droplet-shrinkage process acts directly on the targets of interest.

Conclusions
In this work, we proposed an IME-DS-LF-SERS method for direct identification of typical and high-risk foodborne pathogens, namely, L. monocytogenes. A continuous two-step enrichment by coupling immunomagnetic separation and droplet-shrinkage-induced coagulation of bacteria and plasmonic nanoparticles were achieved and employed for label-free SERS fingerprinting of L. monocytogenes. To the best of our knowledge, the obtained SERS fingerprints were first explored for identifying and quantifying L. monocytogenes. The coagulation behavior was dominated by a solvent evaporation process, and the final complex was confirmed as dispersive bacterial cells enclosed with abundant plasmonic nanoparticles. Under laser radiation, the nanoparticle-rich bacterial cells produced extremely high Raman fingerprint signals. Citrate-coated AgNPs exhibited the strongest Raman fingerprint signals compared with citrate coated AuNPs and ascorbic acid-coated Au@AgNPs. Both suspension composition and nanoparticle concentration had noticeable effect on final Raman fingerprint signal intensity. Using streptavidin modified magnetic beads to conjugate biotinylated anti-L. monocytogenes antibodies, we successfully prepared a highly specific capture bead for bacterial enrichment. The IMBs showed limited cross reactivity with other common nontargets. Coupling IME with DS-LF-SERS enhanced the sensitivity of this method, and the detectable bacterial cell concentration of this method was as low as 7 CFU/mL. Further, using the IME-DS-LF-SERS method obviously reduced the bacteria pre-enrichment time to approximately only 1 h much lower than the conventional plate counting method and sequence-based methods. Multiple Raman fingerprints ensured the method accuracy, overcoming the drawbacks of false positive detection in a conventional immunoassay. This IME-DS-LF-SERS fingerprinting method realized a rapid and quantitative identification of L. monocytogenes in real chicken samples, demonstrating its promising potential for food pathogen monitoring. Therefore, this method has great potential in monitoring pathogen residues in food safety.
