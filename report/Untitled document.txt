1.1 Introduction
Sepsis is a life-threatening clinical syndrome caused by a dysregulated host response to infection that results in organ dysfunction and, in severe cases, septic shock or death. It represents one of the most urgent challenges in modern healthcare due to its rapid progression, high mortality, and significant treatment burden. According to global estimates, sepsis affects millions of patients annually and remains a major cause of preventable mortality, particularly in neonates, pediatric populations, elderly patients, and immunocompromised individuals [1], [4].
The clinical challenge of sepsis is not only its severity, but also the difficulty of early recognition. Initial symptoms may be non-specific and overlap with other inflammatory or physiological conditions. In neonates, warning signs such as poor feeding, lethargy, temperature instability, apnea, or respiratory distress may appear subtle despite serious underlying infection. Delayed diagnosis may rapidly lead to multi-organ dysfunction, prolonged intensive care admission, neurological injury, or death.
Early and appropriate antimicrobial treatment is strongly associated with improved outcomes. However, indiscriminate antibiotic use contributes to antimicrobial resistance (AMR), unnecessary drug exposure, microbiome disruption, and increased healthcare costs. Therefore, clinicians require diagnostic tools that can support rapid yet reliable early decision-making.
This thesis addresses that need through a biomarker-focused Raman spectroscopy framework, with future translation toward a portable SERS-based infection screening platform.

1.2 Need for Improved Diagnostics
An ideal infection diagnostic platform, particularly for suspected sepsis, should satisfy the following criteria:
* Rapid turnaround time (minutes rather than days)
* High sensitivity at low bacterial burden
* High specificity for bacterial infection
* Ability to indicate Gram-positive vs Gram-negative origin
* Low sample volume requirement (critical for neonatal use)
* Minimal sample preparation
* Portability for bedside or low-resource settings
* Affordable and scalable deployment
* Clinically interpretable output
No currently dominant diagnostic approach simultaneously fulfills all of these requirements. Existing methods involve trade-offs between speed, accuracy, infrastructure, cost, and interpretability.

1.3.1 Blood Culture (Current Gold Standard)
Blood culture remains the accepted reference standard for bloodstream infection diagnosis. Patient blood is incubated in growth media, and microbial growth is detected over time. Positive cultures enable organism identification and antibiotic susceptibility testing.
Strengths
* Clinically accepted gold standard
* Detects viable organisms
* Enables targeted therapy
* Supports antimicrobial stewardship when positive
Limitations
Slow Turnaround Time
Detection often requires 24–72 hours or longer, depending on organism type and bacterial load [6], [7].
Low Sensitivity in Low-Biomass Samples
Bacterial concentrations in blood may be very low, especially in early infection or neonatal samples.
Reduced Yield After Antibiotics
Prior antimicrobial exposure may suppress growth and generate false negatives.
Infrastructure Requirement
Requires incubators, microbiology laboratories, trained staff, and continuous workflows.
Clinical Impact
While accurate when positive, blood culture may not provide results quickly enough for early intervention decisions.

1.3.2 Molecular Diagnostics (PCR, qPCR, Multiplex Panels)
Molecular assays detect bacterial nucleic acids directly from patient samples and can reduce time-to-result compared with culture.
Strengths
* Faster than culture
* High analytical sensitivity
* Useful for difficult-to-culture organisms
* Resistance genes may be detectable in some platforms
Limitations
Detection of Non-Viable Organisms
DNA may persist after bacterial death; therefore, positivity does not always imply active infection.
Target Restriction
Many panels detect only predefined pathogens or resistance markers.
High Cost
Instrumentation, cartridges, and consumables may limit widespread adoption.
Technical Complexity
Requires contamination control, trained operators, and robust laboratory workflows.
________________


1.3.3 Host Biomarker Tests (CRP, PCT, IL-6, TNF-α)
Inflammatory biomarkers are widely used as supportive indicators of infection or systemic inflammation.
Strengths
* Rapid assays available
* Common in hospital workflows
* Useful for monitoring inflammatory trends
Limitations
Limited Specificity
These biomarkers may rise in trauma, surgery, autoimmune disease, or sterile inflammation.
No Direct Pathogen Information
They do not identify bacterial structure or Gram class.
Variable Kinetics
Different markers rise and fall at different times, complicating interpretation.
Thus, host biomarkers are valuable supportive tools, but not direct bacterial detection tools.
________________


1.3.4 Raman Spectroscopy and Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS)
Raman spectroscopy is a vibrational technique based on inelastic scattering of light. Molecular bonds produce characteristic energy shifts, generating a spectral fingerprint. Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS) uses plasmonic substrates (e.g., silver or gold nanostructures) to amplify weak Raman signals.
Strengths
* Label-free molecular detection
* Rapid measurement (seconds to minutes)
* Potentially low sample requirement
* Multiplex molecular information in one spectrum
* Strong future portability potential
Current Limitations
* Weak native Raman signals (without enhancement)
* Signal variability due to substrate effects
* Complex spectra in biological fluids
* Need for preprocessing and advanced analytics
* Requires clinical validation against gold standards
Despite these challenges, Raman/SERS offers a promising pathway for next-generation infection diagnostics.

3.1 Introduction
The objective of this thesis is to establish a scientifically validated framework for rapid bacterial biomarker detection using Raman spectroscopy and to define a translational pathway toward a future portable Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS)-based infection screening platform. The work is intentionally structured in phases to separate completed foundational research from ongoing optimization and future product development.
The current phase focuses on three essential tasks:
1. Detect biologically relevant bacterial biomarkers using Raman spectroscopy
2. Validate spectral signatures using Density Functional Theory (DFT)
3. Build a robust analytical workflow for reproducible interpretation
The future phase extends this foundation toward sensitivity enhancement, intelligent analytics, and clinical translation.
To comply with thesis requirements, the research objectives are consolidated into four focused and measurable objectives.

To perform DFT-based vibrational simulations of selected biomarkers or representative structural fragments and compare theoretical frequencies with experimental Raman peaks.

* fluorescence background

* intensity variability
